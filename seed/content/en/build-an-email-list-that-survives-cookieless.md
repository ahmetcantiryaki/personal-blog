Short answer: Chrome abandoned its plan to fully remove third-party cookies back in 2025, but Safari and Firefox already block them by default, and AI Overviews are quietly absorbing search clicks that used to land on your site — so in 2026, your growth engine needs to run through a first-party channel you fully control, which means email.

## Are third-party cookies actually going away?

No — Google reversed course in 2025 and decided to keep third-party cookies on by default in Chrome, managing them through existing privacy settings instead of a new consent prompt. But that's not a return to the old tracking status quo: Safari and Firefox already block third-party cookies by default, Chrome's Incognito mode does the same, and ad identity resolution keeps getting more fragmented across the ecosystem. Fragmented tracking infrastructure doesn't reduce your dependence on first-party data — the data a user hands you directly, like an email address, a stated preference, or observed behavior — it increases it.

Layer search behavior on top of that: AI Overviews and chat assistants now answer the question directly instead of sending the user to click through to your site. If your Search Console data shows impressions in the position 8–12 band with close to zero clicks, that's exactly the query type AI is answering instead of linking out. As that unclicked band of organic traffic shrinks, your email list stays the one channel you can reach directly, independent of any algorithm or summarization-engine change.

## Which lead magnets still convert in 2026?

Tools, templates, and cohort-based email series convert far better than static PDFs, because they deliver immediate, reusable value. A tired "10-Page Guide" PDF is now a weak offer — people download it and never open it again. A calculator, a checklist tool, or a same-day cohort series (like "Launch Your First Campaign in 7 Days") builds commitment instead, because the user sees value right away and has to stay subscribed to get the rest of the series.

| Lead magnet type | Typical conversion behavior | Why it works or doesn't |
|---|---|---|
| Static PDF guide | Low reopen rate, one-time download | Value is consumed instantly, no repeat engagement |
| Calculator/template tool | Repeat use, gets shared | Delivers ongoing practical value |
| Cohort email series | High early open rates | Structure forces continued subscription to finish |
| Webinar recording | Moderate, topic-dependent | One-off unless paired with a follow-up series |

## Do you actually need double opt-in?

Yes, especially if you sell into the European market: double opt-in confirms the subscriber actually provided their own email address, and that protects your deliverability score over the long run. Single opt-in lists grow faster in the short term but fill up with fake or mistyped addresses, which generate soft bounces that drag down your email service provider's sender reputation. As we cover in our [email deliverability checklist](/en/posts/email-deliverability-checklist), once reputation drops, recovering it can take weeks — making double opt-in's small short-term growth cost cheap relative to the long-term deliverability payoff.

## What should replace long signup forms?

Progressive profiling: ask for only an email address on the first form, then collect additional data piece by piece through later interactions — a click, a short survey, a purchase. Asking for eight fields on a signup form measurably drops conversion compared to a single-field form; every extra field raises the odds someone abandons it. Progressive profiling collects the same data over time while keeping initial friction at a minimum.

## How much does segmentation actually lift open and click rates?

Segmentation lifts engagement by sending different content based on behavior instead of blasting the same email to everyone on the list. According to 2026 benchmarks, general campaign emails average a 20.73% open rate and a 2.27% click-through rate, while automated/triggered flows (welcome series, abandoned cart) reach 30.63% open and 7.39% CTR. That gap isn't random: triggered emails go out based on what the user just did, so relevance is inherently higher.

## Which segmentation criteria actually move the needle?

Four criteria make most of the practical difference: signup source (which lead magnet brought them in), behavioral engagement (open/click frequency over the last 30 days), purchase history (never purchased, one-time, or repeat), and stated interest (a topic picked on the signup form or in a preference center). Combining these four and assigning each segment its own send frequency and content type produces meaningfully higher engagement than a single "same weekly newsletter for everyone" approach — educational content aimed at someone who's never purchased shouldn't carry the same message as an upgrade offer aimed at a repeat customer.

One practical warning: over-segmenting can backfire too. Building dozens of micro-segments with only a few hundred people each multiplies your content production load while the returns taper off fast. Starting with four core segments — new subscriber, active, inactive/re-engagement, and customer — and only splitting further when the data actually shows different behavior is the more sustainable approach.

## How fast should a welcome sequence prove its value?

The first email should land within hours of signup and deliver the promised value immediately — a discount code, a template, a first real tip. A delayed or weak first email gives a subscriber who hasn't built trust yet a reason to disengage or, worse, mark it as spam. As we show in our guide on [running email marketing with ChatGPT and Gemini](/en/posts/email-marketing-with-chatgpt-and-gemini), drafting a welcome series with AI is fast, but actually keeping the promise it makes still falls on a human editor.

## How do you measure whether your list is actually healthy?

Track four metrics on a regular cadence: deliverability (inbox versus spam placement), open rate, click rate, and churn rate. Mark any address that hasn't opened anything in 90 days as inactive and move it into a separate re-engagement campaign, then remove whoever still doesn't respond. Keeping dead addresses on your list doesn't just sit there harmlessly — it puts your sender reputation at unnecessary risk.

My own take: most teams treat list size as a success metric, but a dead list of 50,000 is worth less than an engaged list of 5,000. Your ESP typically prices by send volume, so holding onto dead addresses isn't just a reputation risk — it's a direct cost line.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Did third-party cookies actually disappear in 2026?

No. Google abandoned its plan to fully remove third-party cookies in Chrome in 2025 and kept them on by default. Safari and Firefox already block them by default, though, so dependence on first-party data keeps rising regardless.

### Does double opt-in slow down list growth?

Yes, it slows short-term growth slightly because the subscriber has to take a second step to confirm their email address. But that step filters out fake and mistyped addresses, protecting your deliverability score over the long run.

### What's a normal open rate and click rate for an email list in 2026?

2026 benchmarks put general campaign average open rates around 20–31% and click-through rates around 2–4%. Automated, triggered email flows perform well above those campaign averages.

### When should I remove dead email addresses from my list?

Move any address inactive for 90 days into a separate re-engagement campaign first; remove anyone who still doesn't respond to that. Holding onto dead addresses drags down sender reputation and raises your ESP costs unnecessarily.
