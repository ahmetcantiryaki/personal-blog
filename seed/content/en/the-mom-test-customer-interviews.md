Short answer: when you ask a customer "would you buy this?", the "yes" you get back is almost always a lie — a politeness, not a commitment. Rob Fitzpatrick's "Mom Test" method fixes this by having you ask people about their own lives and past behavior instead of your idea, because you learn the truth about someone by listening to them, not by pitching them.

## What is the Mom Test?

Short answer: the Mom Test is a question-asking method built on three core rules: talk about their life instead of your idea, ask about specific past behavior instead of opinions, and talk less so you can listen more. It's named for the fact that even your own mother will lie to you about your idea out of love — unless you ask questions good enough that she can't.

The principle Fitzpatrick keeps returning to in the book is this: the customer owns the problem, you own the solution. When a customer asks for a specific feature, your job isn't to build exactly what they described — it's to find the real motivation underneath the request.

## Why does "would you buy this?" produce a false positive?

Short answer: the moment you describe your idea, the person stops sharing their real experience and starts evaluating your pitch instead — and politeness pushes most people toward a positive evaluation. That's the exact moment a conversation gets corrupted: answers you get before you reveal your idea are real; answers you get after are mostly courtesy.

Compliments are nearly worthless for this reason. Fitzpatrick calls them "the fool's gold of customer learning" — they shine, but they're not valuable. When someone says "this is a great idea," that's not information. It's a social nicety.

| Wrong question (about your idea) | Mom Test question (about their life) |
|---|---|
| "Would you use something like this?" | "What did you actually do the last time this problem came up?" |
| "Would you pay $20/month for this?" | "What are you spending right now — money or time — to deal with this?" |
| "Would this feature be useful to you?" | "Why do you need that feature — what problem does it solve?" |
| "Do you like my idea?" | "When's the last time you talked to someone else about this?" |

## What questions actually work?

Short answer: questions that work ask people to recount what they actually did in the past, not to predict the future or evaluate your idea. "Would you do X in the future?" is a prediction, and predictions are usually wrong; "What did you do the last time you ran into this problem?" is a fact, and facts don't lie.

Time and money spent are far more reliable signals than stated intent. Finding out that someone is already paying for some existing workaround — a spreadsheet, an agency, a competing tool — is far more valuable than hearing "I'd be interested," because the first is observed behavior and the second is a statement of intent.

## What red flags should you watch for during an interview?

Short answer: there are three red flags — compliments, generalizations, and future promises. When someone says "that would be great," "people would love this," or "I'd definitely buy that," none of these is a signal; it's a cue to dig deeper, not a conclusion to record.

Generalizations like "people usually want this" are equally useless without asking who, when, and under what specific circumstance. Fitzpatrick's fix is to reduce every generalization down to one concrete example: "When was the last time that happened?"

## How do you turn interview answers into a decision?

Short answer: never decide based on a single interview — a pattern exists only once you hear the same concrete behavior (same problem, same current workaround, same spending) independently from several different people. You also need to know what you're trying to learn before you walk into the conversation; Fitzpatrick's warning is direct: if you don't know what you're trying to learn, the conversation isn't worth having.

A practical method: after each interview, answer three questions — is this person actually spending time or money on this problem right now, why are they dissatisfied with their current workaround, and does this interview confirm the same pattern as previous ones or does it point somewhere different. A pattern repeating across three or more independent interviews is a real signal; one person's enthusiastic answer is not.

My own take: the Mom Test's harshest and most accurate idea is that your customers don't "want" to lie to you, but they're wired to be polite, and as long as you keep asking questions that give them room to be polite instead of honest, you'll keep getting lied to. Some of the book's specific tactics (like arranging an interview under a different pretext) can feel a bit forced in practice, but the core principle — ask about their life, not your idea — applies to nearly every customer conversation.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### What is the Mom Test?

The Mom Test is a customer-interview method developed by Rob Fitzpatrick that has you ask people about their own lives and past behavior instead of your idea. The goal is to collect real, verifiable information instead of the false-positive answers politeness tends to produce.

### What questions should I avoid in a customer interview?

Avoid questions that ask someone to directly evaluate your idea or predict the future, like "Would you buy this?" or "Do you like this idea?" Instead, ask what they actually did the last time this problem came up and what they're currently spending — time or money — to deal with it.

### Why shouldn't I trust customer compliments?

Rob Fitzpatrick describes compliments as "the fool's gold of customer learning" because people say positive things to be polite, not because they reflect a real commitment. Someone saying "great idea" doesn't mean they'll ever spend time or money on it.

### How many interviews does it take to validate an idea?

There's no fixed number, but you have a real signal once you hear the same concrete behavior pattern (same problem, same current workaround, same spending) consistently across several independent interviews. One person's enthusiastic answer is never sufficient evidence on its own.
