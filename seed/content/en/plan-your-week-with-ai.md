Short answer: set aside 30 minutes on Friday, dump every task and note into a Claude or ChatGPT Project, ask the assistant to cluster them by priority and effort, then place the resulting clusters on your calendar as real time blocks. The step that actually makes this work is turning it into a ritual repeated every week inside the same Project, not a one-off chat.

## How Does the "Friday 30" Ritual Actually Work?

The ritual has three steps: dump, cluster, time-block. First, you dump every open task, half-finished note, and known commitment for the coming week into one message to the assistant — raw, with no attempt to organize it yourself. The assistant sorts that raw list into clusters by importance and required effort; you then place those clusters into the open slots on your calendar by hand. Doing this on Friday clears your head before the weekend and means you start Monday with a plan already in place.

The real advantage of doing this inside a Project is that you don't have to re-explain yourself every Friday. Claude Projects are now free for all users, and free accounts can create up to five Projects; a Project bundles instructions, a knowledge base of files, and your past chats into one persistent workspace. Once your instructions file says "every Friday, cluster my tasks by importance and effort, output in the format below," you stop writing the same prompt from scratch every week.

## How Do You Ask It to Cluster Tasks by Priority and Effort?

Giving the assistant a clear clustering framework when you hand over your raw task list is what makes the output actually usable. One that works well: high importance + low effort (do it now), high importance + high effort (time-block it), low importance + low effort (batch into one session), low importance + high effort (defer or cut it). Asking for these four buckets moves you from "what should I do today" to "when should I block this cluster."

| Cluster | Definition | Where it goes on the calendar |
| --- | --- | --- |
| High importance, low effort | Quick to do, high impact | First thing Monday morning |
| High importance, high effort | Big work needing focus | A fixed 90-minute block |
| Low importance, low effort | Small admin tasks | One "batch work" block |
| Low importance, high effort | Questionable value, high cost | Defer it or cut it from the list |

## How Do You Time-Block the Resulting Plan on Your Calendar?

Rather than copying the assistant's clustered list straight onto your calendar, you need to carve out a real slot for every high-effort cluster — "I'll get to it sometime" is the exact opposite of time-blocking. The practical rule: every task in the high-importance, high-effort cluster gets its own named block of at least 60–90 minutes on the calendar; low-effort tasks get squeezed into a single "batch work" block instead. As we covered in [our guide to beating procrastination with systems that stick](/en/posts/beat-procrastination-systems-that-stick), a task that shows up on the calendar by name gets finished at a far higher rate than one that just sits on a list.

## How Do You Stop the Assistant From Inventing Commitments?

When you hand over a raw task list, the assistant can sometimes fill a gap with its own guess and invent a commitment that was never there — adding, say, "this probably needs prep work too" for something you never actually asked for. The clearest fix is telling the instructions file explicitly, "only cluster the tasks I give you, don't add tasks on your own assumption," and then checking every line the assistant added against where it actually came from in your notes when you review the output.

```text
Project instructions template:
"Sort the raw task list I give you into these four clusters:
1) High importance + low effort
2) High importance + high effort
3) Low importance + low effort
4) Low importance + high effort
Use only the tasks I give you — do not add tasks based on your own guess.
State the number of tasks and estimated time per cluster."
```

## What Should the Reflection Prompt That Closes the Week Look Like?

At the end of the week, going back to the same Project and asking "which cluster did I estimate correctly this week, which one did I get wrong" sharpens next week's clustering instructions. This follows the same principle we recommended in [our guide to writing a self-review and brag doc with AI](/en/posts/write-self-review-brag-doc-with-ai): gather evidence weekly, synthesize monthly — a single weekly checkpoint makes your planning accuracy measurable over time instead of staying a vague feeling.

## Should You Pick ChatGPT or Claude for This?

Either one is capable enough for this ritual, but each has different strengths. ChatGPT is stronger at research, image generation, and voice interaction; Claude is stronger at long-context reasoning and strategic pushback — actually telling you where your plan is weak. For weekly planning specifically, the real difference isn't which tool you pick — it's whether you set that tool up as a Project, write the instructions once, and actually reuse it every week.

My honest take: the hard part of this ritual isn't picking the right tool — it's the discipline. Thirty minutes on Friday costs far less than starting Monday morning with no plan at all, but it only works if you actually do it every single week.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Are Claude Projects and ChatGPT Projects free for weekly planning?

Claude Projects are now free for all users, with free accounts able to create up to five Projects. ChatGPT's equivalent Project feature is also usable on free accounts in its basic form, though expanded memory and file capacity are larger on paid plans.

### Can the assistant prepare my plan automatically every Monday?

Not directly — Claude and ChatGPT Projects don't run on their own schedule by default. If you want an automatic trigger like "every Monday" or "whenever an email arrives," you need to connect the assistant through an automation tool like Zapier or Make.

### How do I stop the assistant from inventing a task that doesn't exist?

Tell the Project's instructions explicitly to "use only the tasks I give you, don't add tasks based on your own guess," and check every item in the output against where it actually came from in your notes. That largely stops the assistant from filling gaps with its own assumptions.

### Is Claude or ChatGPT better for weekly planning?

Either works; ChatGPT is stronger at research and voice interaction, Claude is stronger at long-context reasoning and pointing out weaknesses in your plan. The real difference comes from setting the tool up as a Project and writing the instructions once, not from which one you choose.
