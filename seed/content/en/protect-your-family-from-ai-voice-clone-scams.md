Short answer: the most reliable defense against an AI voice-clone scam is a safe word your family agrees on in advance, combined with hanging up on any "emergency" call — no matter how familiar the voice sounds — and calling the person back on a number you already know is theirs. The FTC's official warning notes that a scammer can build a convincing clone from just a few seconds of audio pulled off social media, which is why protecting your family is now a matter of a procedure you set up ahead of time, not a technical fix.

## How do you stop an AI voice-clone scam as a family?

Two steps are enough: first, hang up and call the person back on a known, previously saved number — never the number the caller gave you. Second, ask for a safe word your family agreed on in advance; a real family member knows it, and an AI clone can't, because the word was never written down, shared digitally, or spoken into any recording. The FTC lists both steps as the core defense in its warning on family emergency scams.

## How do you set up and use a family safe word?

Agree on the word in person, during a family conversation — never over text, email, or social media, because any written or spoken trace becomes information a scammer could obtain. Keep it simple: a memorable but unguessable word or short phrase is enough. Teach every family member the same rule: any call or message claiming an emergency gets asked for the word, and no money or information moves until it's given. Changing the word once a year adds another layer of protection against the risk that it leaked to someone outside the family.

## How do you limit your voice samples on social media?

All a scammer needs is a few seconds of public audio — any Instagram Reel, TikTok clip, or voice note shared as a WhatsApp status qualifies. Practical steps: switch public posts and videos to a restricted audience; check specifically whether older relatives' profiles are public; make text the default communication method instead of voice messages. This doesn't mean hiding your voice entirely — a realistic goal is reducing the amount of public audio a random scammer could easily find.

| Measure | Priority for | Effect |
|---|---|---|
| Family safe word | Everyone | The single most reliable defense |
| Callback verification | Everyone | Neutralizes caller-ID spoofing |
| Social media privacy settings | Anyone with a public profile | Reduces attack material, doesn't eliminate it |
| Pre-arranged bank verification | Anyone who moves money | Prevents large losses |
| Dedicated training for older relatives | Family members 65+ | Critical for the most-targeted group |

## How do you set up callback verification with your bank?

Most banks offer a separate verification step for large or unusual transfers — set this up ahead of time by calling your bank, not in the middle of a scam attempt. Ask for three things: extra confirmation via SMS or app for large transfers, an automatic hold window on suspicious transactions (usually a right to cancel within the first 24 hours), and a "trusted contact" record — a mechanism that lets the bank verify with a relative of the account holder too. Setting these up in advance is far more reliable than inventing a new procedure in the middle of a panic.

## How do you teach older relatives about this?

Older adults are the most frequent target of this scam because "grandchild in trouble" or "my child was in an accident" scenarios directly exploit parental and grandparental instinct. Teaching should rest on three simple rules: (1) no real emergency demands payment immediately and only via gift cards or cryptocurrency; (2) asking for the safe word is never rude — it's a normal family security step; (3) hanging up and calling another family member when in doubt is always safe, because a few minutes of delay rarely matters in a genuine emergency. Explaining this once isn't enough — repeat it once a year with a concrete example.

## What should you do in the first 24 hours if you're targeted?

The first step is to call the bank or payment provider you sent money through immediately; some transfers can still be stopped within the first few hours. The second step is filing an official report at ReportFraud.ftc.gov — this creates your own record and helps authorities track repeating attack patterns. The third step is warning other family members who could be targeted with the same method, since scammers typically reuse the same script against multiple people. ## Is the video-call deepfake risk different from voice cloning?

Yes, and the risk is growing. Voice cloning alone is enough for a phone call, but real-time video deepfakes can now produce a fake face that looks "live" on a video meeting — which makes a second verification layer even more important, especially for corporate CEO fraud. The practical defense is the same principle applied to video: when a large financial request comes up on a video call, end the call and reach the same person again through a channel you already know is theirs, such as an internal company messaging app. A video looking "live" and "real-time" is no longer a verification on its own.

## How often should you test your family's safe word?

Running a small drill at least once a year, like a real scenario, works well: one family member calls another with a fake "emergency" script, and you check whether the safe word gets asked for in the right order. This drill is especially useful for checking whether older relatives actually remember the procedure under simulated pressure — and it often reveals that someone skipped a step on the first try, giving you a chance to fix it before a real attack happens.
