Short answer: spend two weeks validating before you write a single line of your micro-SaaS — 10 Mom Test interviews, then a landing-page smoke test, then a manual concierge pilot with your strongest lead. If none of the three produce a real commitment signal, kill the idea and move on.

Most solo founders skip this because validating doesn't feel like progress. Building does. That instinct is exactly what turns a weekend side project into three wasted months, because code is the most expensive way to find out nobody wanted the thing.

## What Makes a Micro-SaaS Problem Worth Building?

A problem is worth building for when people are already paying for a bad solution or already burning real time on a workaround — a spreadsheet nobody trusts, a Slack thread that re-answers the same question weekly, a Zapier chain held together with duct tape. That existing spend, in money or time, is evidence. A problem someone merely finds "annoying" when you describe it is not.

The niche part matters as much as the pain part. "Project management" is not a niche; "scheduling for 10-person dental practices that still fax insurance claims" is. A narrow niche means you can find all 10 interview candidates in a single online community, and it means your landing page can speak so specifically to one kind of reader that generic visitors bounce and the right ones convert.

## What Is the Mom Test, and How Do You Run 10 Interviews With It?

The Mom Test, from Rob Fitzpatrick's book of the same name, is a simple filter: would this question give you useful data even from your mom, who loves you and will lie to make you feel good? Most pitch-style questions fail that test immediately.

Three rules keep an interview honest. First, ask about their life, not your idea — "walk me through the last time this happened" instead of "would you use a tool that does X." Second, ask about specific past events, not predictions; people are bad forecasters of their own future behavior but accurate narrators of what they actually did last Tuesday. Third, talk less than they do, and listen for commitment rather than compliments — money already spent, time already invested, or a workaround they built themselves are real signals. "That's such a great idea" is not.

| Signal | What it looks like | How much to trust it |
|---|---|---|
| Compliment | "I'd definitely use that!" | Ignore — costs them nothing to say |
| Hypothetical | "I'd probably pay $20/month" | Weak — no past behavior behind it |
| Past spend | "We pay $40/month for a tool we hate" | Strong — proven willingness to pay |
| Built workaround | "I built a spreadsheet that does half of this" | Strong — proven time investment |
| Concierge payment | Paid you directly for the manual version | Strongest — real money, right now |

Line up 10 conversations in your niche community, Slack group, or subreddit before you write any product copy. If more than half surface the same specific, currently-paid-for-or-worked-around problem without you leading them there, you have a real signal.

## How Do You Run a Landing-Page Smoke Test?

A smoke test is one page describing a product that doesn't exist yet, sent to real traffic, measuring one concrete action — an email signup, a waitlist join, or a deposit click — against a bar you set before launch, not after you see the number. Setting the bar afterward just lets you rationalize whatever result shows up.

Conversion benchmarks here are genuinely inconsistent across sources, so treat any number as rough context tied to traffic quality, not a target to hit. Cold traffic (ads, cold outreach, strangers from a general subreddit) commonly lands in roughly the 2–10% range for a real commitment action. Warm traffic — your own list, a community where you're already known, people from your 10 interviews — can run meaningfully higher, sometimes 20–40% or more. The gap between those two ranges is wide enough that comparing your result to someone else's blog post is close to meaningless; compare it only to the bar you picked for your own traffic.

## What Is a Concierge MVP, and Why Run One Before You Build?

A concierge MVP means you personally deliver the outcome by hand — the thing your eventual software will automate — before you write the automation. If your idea is a tool that generates weekly compliance reports, you generate those reports manually for two or three real customers first, by email or spreadsheet, and see if they pay for the outcome.

This step exists because a landing-page signup is cheap to give and easy to regret. A person who hands over a card number, or commits real time to working with you by hand, has crossed a line a hypothetical "I'd pay for that" never does. If nobody will go through the manual version, no amount of good engineering later will fix that.

## What Does a Two-Week Validation Sprint Look Like?

A two-week sprint turns validation from an open-ended, indefinitely-postponable task into something with a deadline and a verdict. The structure below is deliberately tight — each phase either produces a real signal or it doesn't, and day 14 is a decision point, not a suggestion.

| Days | Phase | Goal | Kill signal if it happens |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1–4 | 10 Mom Test interviews | Find one specific, shared, currently-paid-for problem | Every conversation surfaces a different, unrelated problem |
| 5–9 | Landing-page smoke test | Clear the conversion bar you set for your traffic quality | Result badly misses the bar even accounting for traffic source |
| 10–14 | Concierge pilot | Get 1–2 people to pay or commit real time for the manual version | Nobody will pay or commit time for the outcome itself |

## Which Signals Mean You Should Walk Away?

Walk away when the data keeps saying no in a specific way, not when you simply feel discouraged. The four clearest kill signals are: your 10 interviews surface different, unrelated problems instead of one shared pain; everyone compliments the idea but nobody can describe a specific recent instance of the problem; your smoke test misses the pre-set bar by a wide margin even after accounting for traffic quality; and nobody — not one person — will pay or give up their current workaround for the concierge version.

Killing an idea in week two is cheap. Killing it in month three, after you've built the thing, is not — and a validated niche with a working [distribution partnership](/en/posts/distribution-partnerships-solo-founders) is worth more than a clever product nobody asked for. ## Frequently Asked Questions

### How many customer interviews do you need to validate a micro-SaaS idea?

Ten focused, Mom Test-style interviews in your specific niche are usually enough to tell you whether a problem is real and shared. If more than half surface the same specific, currently-paid-for-or-worked-around pain without you leading them there, that's a genuine signal worth testing further.

### What's a good landing-page conversion rate for a validation smoke test?

There's no single good number — cold traffic commonly converts at roughly 2–10% for a real commitment action, while warm traffic from your own network can reach 20–40% or more. Set your own bar before launch based on your actual traffic source, and judge the result against that bar, not against someone else's case study.

### What is a concierge MVP and why do it before building software?

A concierge MVP is you manually delivering the outcome your software will eventually automate, to a handful of real customers, before writing any code. It proves people will pay for the outcome itself, which a landing-page signup alone can't prove, since signing up costs a visitor nothing.

### When should a solo founder kill a micro-SaaS idea instead of building it?

Kill it when your 10 interviews surface different unrelated problems instead of one shared pain, your smoke test badly misses the bar you set for your traffic quality, and nobody will pay or commit real time for the concierge version. Two weeks of no signal is far cheaper than three months of unwanted code.
