Short answer: before you ship an AI feature, get four things sorted — a clear AI-use disclosure, updated terms of service with an acceptable-use clause, a written data-handling understanding with your model vendor, and a liability cap in your contract. EU users add one more check: the EU AI Act's transparency rules, enforceable since August 2, 2026. None of this replaces a lawyer once real money or regulated data is on the line.

This is not legal advice — it's a starting checklist for founders who need to move fast and know what to fix first. Laws differ by jurisdiction: the EU AI Act only binds you if you have EU users or operate in the EU, and the US, UK, and other regimes have their own, often lighter, rules.

## What legal documents do I need before shipping AI?

At minimum: updated Terms of Service, a Privacy Policy naming your AI vendor and what you send it, and an Acceptable Use Policy covering what users may not do (generate illegal content, scrape at scale, reverse-engineer the model). If the feature touches health, legal, or financial topics, add a plain "not medical/legal/financial advice" disclaimer near the output, not buried in a footer link.

Most solo founders skip the Acceptable Use Policy because it feels redundant with the ToS. It isn't — an AUP lets you suspend an account for abuse without renegotiating your whole contract.

## Do I have to tell users a feature is AI-powered?

Yes, in most cases, and it's good practice even where it isn't strictly required. A one-line disclosure at the point of interaction — "This response is generated by AI" — is enough for most limited-risk features like chat widgets or summarizers. For anything that could pass as a human agent, disclosure must happen before the user relies on the output, not after.

A minimal, non-intrusive disclosure banner looks like this:

```text
This feature uses AI to generate responses. Outputs may be
inaccurate — verify before relying on them. Not medical, legal,
or financial advice.
```

Put it where the AI output actually appears, not only in a settings page nobody reads.

## Does the EU AI Act apply to my startup?

If you have no EU users and no EU establishment, the Act does not apply to you directly — but check again once you land your first EU customer, because obligations attach to reach, not headquarters. Article 50's transparency duties became applicable on August 2, 2026: providers of AI systems that interact directly with people (chatbots, voice assistants, AI agents) must disclose that users are talking to an AI, unless that is already obvious from context, and generative AI outputs must be marked in a machine-readable way as AI-generated. Systems already on the market get a transition window for the technical marking requirement, running until December 2, 2026.

Penalties can reach €15 million or 3% of global annual turnover, whichever is higher, so this is not a rule to shrug off if you have any EU traffic. See our [EU AI Act enforcement explainer](/en/posts/eu-ai-act-enforcement-live-2026) for a fuller breakdown of what changed. Outside the EU, expect a patchwork: the US has sector-specific rules and state laws rather than one federal AI statute, and the UK currently leans on existing regulators applying existing law to AI.

## What do vendor and data-sharing terms need to cover?

Read your model provider's API terms before you read your own — your obligations to users often trace straight back to what you agreed to with OpenAI, Anthropic, Google, or whoever you're calling. As of September 2026, the major API providers (unlike their consumer chat apps) generally do not use API-submitted data for training by default: Anthropic's Commercial Terms state it never trains on API traffic, with standard log retention cut to seven days since September 2025, and OpenAI's API excludes customer content from training, with abuse-monitoring retention around 30 days unless you're on a zero-data-retention plan. Confirm the current terms yourself — consumer-tier products can have different defaults than the API.

Your own contract needs to say, in plain terms: which vendor processes user data, what you send them, how long you retain outputs and logs, and whether you sub-process to a fourth party. If you're evaluating whether to build on one model or diversify, our piece on [AI vendor lock-in](/en/posts/ai-vendor-lock-in-startups) is worth reading before you sign anything.

## Who owns the output my AI generates?

In most jurisdictions today, purely AI-generated output without meaningful human authorship is not eligible for copyright protection, so you cannot always assume exclusive ownership of what your feature produces. Your terms should be explicit about what you're actually granting: a license to use the output, not a guarantee of exclusive IP rights you may not legally hold — see [who owns the code your AI wrote](/en/posts/who-owns-code-your-ai-wrote) for the code-specific angle.

## How do I limit liability when the AI gets something wrong?

Cap your liability in the contract, disclaim warranties on AI output accuracy, and never let a chatbot give advice in a regulated category without a disclaimer next to the answer. A standard SaaS limitation-of-liability clause still applies, but AI features add a new failure mode: a hallucinated fact the user acted on. State explicitly, in the terms and near the feature, that outputs are "as is" and should be verified before reliance — especially for health, legal, or financial decisions.

Your privacy practices intersect with liability here too: if the feature processes personal data, your [privacy and data-retention obligations](/en/posts/how-claude-chatgpt-gemini-remember-you) shape your GDPR/CCPA exposure.

## What's the one-page checklist?

| Item | Why it matters | Do now vs. later |
|---|---|---|
| AI-use disclosure at point of interaction | Required by EU AI Act Article 50 for EU users; good practice everywhere | Now |
| Updated Terms of Service + AUP | Lets you suspend abuse, sets usage rules | Now |
| Vendor data-processing terms reviewed | Confirms training/retention defaults, sub-processors | Now |
| Not medical/legal/financial advice disclaimer | Limits liability for regulated-topic outputs | Now |
| Output ownership language in ToS | Sets expectations on IP you may not hold | Now |
| Liability cap and "as is" clause | Bounds downside from AI errors | Now |
| Privacy policy names AI vendor and data flows | GDPR/CCPA transparency requirement | Now |
| Full EU AI Act risk classification | Only needed if your system is higher-risk than "limited risk" | Later, with counsel |
| Sector-specific compliance (health, finance, hiring) | Extra rules apply (e.g., HIPAA, FCRA, EEOC) | Later, with counsel |
| International data transfer mechanism (SCCs) | Needed once you process EU personal data outside the EU | Later, with counsel |

## When do I actually need to call a lawyer?

Call a lawyer before you self-serve if your AI feature touches health, legal, financial, or hiring decisions, processes sensitive personal data at scale, or if you're raising a round and diligence will scrutinize your AI stack. Checklists like this one cover the baseline, not regulated verticals or a term sheet's indemnification clauses. Budget one to two hours of a startup lawyer's time before a major launch — cheaper than fixing a contract after a complaint.

My honest take: most solo founders over-invest in polishing the product and under-invest in the two-hour lawyer call that would catch an indemnification gap or a missing disclosure. Do the checklist yourself first, then buy the hour instead of skipping it entirely.

For the EU AI Act specifically, the [European Commission's guidelines on transparency obligations](https://digital-strategy.ec.europa.eu/en/policies/guidelines-ai-transparency-obligations) and the [Article 50 explainer](https://artificialintelligenceact.eu/article/50/) are worth reading before you talk to counsel, and [Cooley's summary of the August 2026 transparency deadline](https://www.cooley.com/news/insight/2026/2026-08-03-eu-ai-act-transparency-obligations-take-effect-2-august-2026) is a solid plain-English overview from a startup-focused law firm. For vendor terms, read [Anthropic's API and data retention documentation](https://platform.claude.com/docs/en/manage-claude/api-and-data-retention) directly — vendor policies update more often than blog posts about them.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Do I need a lawyer to launch an AI feature as a solo founder?

Not necessarily for a low-risk feature like a summarizer or chat widget aimed at general consumers — a careful self-serve pass on disclosure, terms, and vendor terms covers most early-stage risk. Hire a lawyer once you touch regulated data, hiring decisions, health or financial advice, or before closing a funding round.

### Does the EU AI Act apply if my startup isn't based in the EU?

Yes, if you have users in the EU, the Act generally follows your users' location, not your company's headquarters. The Article 50 transparency duties — disclosing AI interaction and marking AI-generated content — became enforceable on August 2, 2026, and apply regardless of where the provider is incorporated, as long as the system is placed on the EU market or used by people in the EU.

### Does OpenAI or Anthropic use my API data to train their models?

As of September 2026, neither OpenAI's nor Anthropic's standard API terms use customer-submitted data for training by default; this differs from some consumer chat products, which can default to opt-out rather than opt-in. Verify current terms directly on the vendor's site, since policies change.

### What happens if I skip the AI disclosure requirement?

If you have EU users, skipping the Article 50 disclosure exposes you to fines of up to €15 million or 3% of global annual turnover, whichever is higher, once enforcement targets your product. Outside the EU, the immediate risk is more often reputational and contractual — a customer or platform partner finding out later tends to damage trust more than any single regulator does.
