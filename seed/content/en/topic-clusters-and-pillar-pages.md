Short answer: to build a topic cluster, pick one broad pillar topic first, split it into 8-15 concrete subtopics, and link every cluster page to the pillar while the pillar links back to every cluster page. The order matters: writing the pillar first and filling the cluster in afterward carries far less cannibalization risk than working backward — writing scattered articles first and trying to link them into a cluster later.

## What is a topic cluster?

A topic cluster is a hub-and-spoke structure: one pillar page covers a topic broadly, and several cluster pages go deep on its subtopics. The pillar links to every cluster page, and each cluster page links back to the pillar. In [why topical authority is the backbone of 2026 SEO](/en/posts/topical-authority-content-clusters), we covered *why* this model matters and how AI Overviews have changed click-through rates; this guide focuses on *how* to actually build one.

## How do you pick the pillar topic?

The right pillar topic is an area your brand genuinely has expertise in, broad enough to reasonably support at least 8-10 distinct subtopics. "API design" works as a pillar because it carries independent subtopics like versioning, authentication, rate limiting, and documentation; "pagination in REST APIs" is already a narrow cluster-page topic, not a pillar. Per [HubSpot's topic cluster model](https://blog.hubspot.com/marketing/topic-clusters-seo), a pillar page should run 2,500-4,000 words, mapping the topic without going deep on any single subtopic; the job is not to explain everything, it's to show where each subtopic gets explained in depth.

A common mistake at this stage is picking the pillar topic because it has high traffic potential, rather than because your brand actually has depth there. Building a pillar on a high-traffic topic your brand doesn't deeply understand leaves the cluster pages thin, which weakens the authority signal the whole structure is supposed to build — search engines weigh the actual depth of knowledge on your pages, not just how many pages you've published.

## How do you choose cluster pages?

Cluster pages come from the pillar topic's subtopics that map to real search queries. The practical method: search the pillar topic, collect the "people also ask" and related-search sections, then group the recurring themes. That first pass usually produces 20-30 candidate subtopics, and your job is narrowing that list down to the 8-15 that map to real, distinct search intent rather than minor variations of the same question. Each cluster page should run 800-1,500 words and go deep enough on one subtopic to stand on its own — not a thin page that exists only to funnel traffic to the pillar.

| | Pillar Page | Cluster Page |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Word count | 2,500-4,000 | 800-1,500 |
| Scope | Broad, maps the topic | One subtopic, in depth |
| Link direction | Links to every cluster page | Links back to the pillar |
| Update frequency | Often, as the cluster grows | When data or versions change |
| Typical count | 1 | 8-15 |

## What are the internal-linking rules?

Three rules cover it: (1) every cluster page should link to the pillar within its first 150 words, using descriptive anchor text — not "click here," but something like "API versioning strategies"; (2) the pillar page should link to every published cluster page in the body text, not just in a footer list; (3) cluster pages can link to each other, but those links should never outrank the pillar-cluster relationship. Our piece on [API versioning strategies](/en/posts/api-versioning-strategies) links back to its own pillar (API design) this way — one descriptive anchor, inside the opening paragraphs.

## How do you avoid keyword cannibalization?

Cannibalization means two of your own pages compete for the same search query, and it's the most common mistake made while building a cluster. The concrete fix: before writing a cluster page, check how deep your pillar page already goes on that subtopic. If the pillar covers a subtopic in more than 300 words, trim that section down and link out to the cluster page instead — otherwise Google decides which of your two pages ranks, and that choice usually isn't the one that's best for your brand. The same principle applies in [SEO for comparison and alternative pages](/en/posts/comparison-pages-that-rank-convert): one page type should serve one query intent.

## How do you measure whether the cluster is working?

The most reliable way to tell if a cluster is working is to compare organic traffic across the pillar plus every cluster page combined, before the cluster existed and 60-90 days after — not traffic to any single page. HubSpot's 2026 State of AEO report found that pages with outbound links, concrete statistics, author bios, and a visible "last updated" date get cited more often across AI Overviews, Gemini, and Perplexity; adding those four elements to both your pillar and cluster pages gives you an extra signal that's easier to measure alongside raw traffic. [Whitehat SEO's guide to pillar pages](https://www.whitehat-seo.co.uk/blog/what-is-a-pillar-page) reports the same pattern — pages carrying those four elements perform more consistently in both traditional rankings and AI citations — though since that figure comes from an SEO agency rather than a primary study, treat it as a signal to test on your own data, not an absolute rule.

## When should you update the pillar versus a cluster page?

A cluster page needs an update when the data or version number it covers changes — that usually affects one subtopic, so refreshing that single page is enough. The pillar page works differently: every time the cluster grows, meaning a new cluster page gets published, the pillar needs updating too, with a link added to that new page. Skipping this step is the most common maintenance mistake — a new cluster page goes live, but the pillar never gets touched, which leaves the new page's internal-link signal weak for as long as that gap exists.

If you ask me, the most common mistake here is building the cluster backward — writing scattered articles on loosely related topics first, then labeling them a "cluster" after the fact. That raises your internal link count, but it doesn't strengthen the authority signal, because the pillar page itself stays thin. As we noted in [what content decay is and how to fix it](/en/posts/what-is-content-decay-how-to-fix-it), adding links on top of a weak foundation is never a substitute for fixing the foundation itself.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### What's the difference between a pillar page and a cluster page?

A pillar page covers a topic broadly in 2,500-4,000 words and serves as the hub; a cluster page goes deep on one subtopic in 800-1,500 words and serves as a spoke. The pillar links to every cluster page, and each cluster page links back to the pillar.

### How many cluster pages does a pillar need?

The practical range is 8-15 cluster pages. Fewer than that usually means the topic isn't covered deep enough; significantly more often means the topic has been split into pieces that are too narrow, which raises cannibalization risk.

### Should you write cluster pages before or after the pillar?

Write the pillar page first, then add cluster pages one at a time. Working in the opposite order — publishing scattered articles first and trying to link them into a cluster afterward — increases both cannibalization risk and inconsistency between pages.

### How long does a topic cluster take to show results?

A cluster's full effect is usually measurable within 60-90 days, since search engines need time to crawl the new link structure and re-evaluate the authority signal. Don't draw conclusions before that window has passed after publishing the first cluster pages.
