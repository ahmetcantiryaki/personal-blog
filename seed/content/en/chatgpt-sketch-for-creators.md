It's 11 p.m. and you're building a carousel. You know exactly where the headline box should sit and where the logo goes, but every time you type it out, ChatGPT puts the logo on the wrong side. Short answer: skip the words, draw a rough box layout instead, type "@Sketch," and ChatGPT uses your doodle as the layout reference for the final image. This feature shipped as part of ChatGPT Images 2.5, which OpenAI released on September 8, 2026.

## What is ChatGPT Sketch?

ChatGPT Sketch is an Images 2.5 feature that lets you draw directly inside the chat window and use that drawing as a visual reference for image generation. You can sketch the rough shape of a room, the silhouette of an outfit, or the layout of a social post, and the model treats your drawing as the composition to follow.

You invoke it by typing "@Sketch" in the message box, or by picking Sketch from the "+" menu. A canvas opens where you draw with a mouse or touch, roughly blocking out a headline area, a logo circle, a text block at the bottom. According to OpenAI's announcement, that rough drawing becomes the direct reference for the final composition, so the model no longer has to guess at an abstract layout you tried to describe in words; it follows the shape you actually drew. TechRepublic's coverage frames Sketch as one of the most literal "point-and-mark" steps ChatGPT has added to image editing.

Sending the sketch is not the end of the process. If the first result is off, you keep iterating with follow-up prompts like "make the box in the top right bigger." Images 2.5 follows multi-turn editing instructions more reliably than the previous model, so a run of five or six small corrections in a row does not undo the changes you made earlier in the same session.

## How do you actually use Sketch?

The most practical way to use Sketch in daily content production is a three-step loop: sketch the layout, generate, then refine with follow-up prompts. That loop is what saves time on repeat formats like thumbnails, carousels, and post graphics.

1. **Sketch the layout:** Use boxes and rough shapes to mark where text goes and where the hero element sits. You do not need drawing skill; the goal is to show proportions and hierarchy.
2. **Generate with @Sketch:** Submit the drawing along with a short text instruction, such as "YouTube thumbnail, bold yellow headline, gradient background." Images 2.5 brings a noticeable jump in natural lighting and texture quality over the previous model, so the first output usually looks better than expected.
3. **Refine with follow-up prompts:** Fix color, font weight, or element size one at a time in new messages. The model retains the original sketch and the edits you've already made.

This loop pays off most in carousel series: sketch the first slide's layout once, lock the composition, then carry that same skeleton across the rest of the deck by saying "keep the same layout, change the headline." It builds on the GPT-Image-based carousel workflow covered in our [guide to making social graphics with AI image tools](/en/posts/make-social-graphics-with-ai-image-tools), with Sketch adding a new layer on top.

## How do you keep outputs on-brand?

Keeping outputs on-brand means repeating the same reference palette and font instruction in every generation; Sketch locks the layout, but color and typography decisions are still yours to make. Stating your brand colors as hex codes and naming your font at the start of a session, then repeating a short reminder in each prompt, produces the most consistent results.

Consistency matters more in graphics that include text, like headlines, button labels, or quote cards. Images 2.5's text handling is more reliable than the prior version, but you still need to spell out the same font and capitalization rules every time; otherwise the model can pick a different typographic style on each generation. The same applies to reference images: when you upload a previously approved post design, Images 2.5 preserves the identity of subjects in that reference better than before, which helps with recurring elements like a logo or a mascot.

## How much faster is Sketch than prompt-only generation?

Sketch saves time by removing the step of describing composition entirely in words; the real gain is fewer back-and-forth rounds spent saying "no, make the logo bigger." Prompt-only generation typically takes three to four rounds to nail a layout; with Sketch, that usually drops to one or two rounds because the layout is already visually defined.

On top of that, OpenAI reported that image generation latency — the time between sending a request and getting a result — dropped by up to 50% compared with the previous Images 2.0 model. Fewer rounds plus faster rounds compound, which adds up to real time savings when you're producing graphics on a tight content calendar.

## Does a designer still win at anything here?

Yes, a real designer still wins on brand-system consistency at scale and on anything requiring print or vector output. Sketch is great for producing one graphic quickly, but it does not guarantee pixel alignment, color-profile accuracy, or vector-based logo files across dozens of formats.

My honest take: for fast, one-off social graphics — a thumbnail test, a story background, a quote card — Sketch is now a more sensible option than hiring a junior designer, because the speed and cost gap is too large to ignore. But for corporate work where a brand kit has to stay identical across print, packaging, and multiple platforms, no image-generation model fully replaces a human designer's ability to produce vector files, manage color profiles, and follow a brand guideline to the letter.

Sketch outputs are also still raster images, meaning they lose quality when scaled up. For print jobs, large-format posters, or logo work that needs SVG files, this feature is not the right tool.

## How do you disclose AI-generated graphics honestly?

Being honest with your audience means clearly labeling graphics that are entirely AI-generated, especially when they could be mistaken for a real person, product, or event. Most platforms, including Instagram, LinkedIn, and YouTube, now offer AI content labeling options; the bar is lower for purely design-oriented graphics like carousels or thumbnails that don't mislead anyone, but staying transparent when followers ask keeps trust intact.

If you're sharing something resembling a real product photo or a realistic depiction of a person, disclosing that it's AI-assisted is both the ethical move and increasingly expected under platform policy. ## Frequently Asked Questions

### How do you turn on ChatGPT Sketch?

Type "@Sketch" in the message box, or select Sketch from the "+" menu. A canvas opens where you can draw with a mouse or touch input, then attach that drawing to your image generation request.

### Is ChatGPT Sketch free to use?

Sketch is not a separate paid add-on; it ships as part of the Images 2.5 model, available to ChatGPT, ChatGPT Work, and Codex users as of September 2026. Usage still falls under your existing ChatGPT plan limits.

### What kinds of images work best with Sketch?

Images with a clear composition — carousels with a defined layout, thumbnails where headline and text placement matter, post graphics where element position is important — get the most benefit from Sketch. Free-form, composition-agnostic artistic images show less of a difference, since prompt-only generation already handles those well.

### Does Sketch replace design tools like Photoshop or Figma?

No, Sketch is a shortcut for fast sketch-to-image generation; it does not offer layer-based editing, vector work, or print-ready file output. Professional brand systems and multi-format design work still require tools like Figma, Photoshop, or Illustrator.
