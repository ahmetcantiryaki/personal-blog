Short answer: keep a running "brag document" that you update months before your performance review, not the week of it. Log what you shipped, the measurable impact, and the feedback you received as it happens. When review time comes, you work from a written record instead of trying to reconstruct six months of work from memory.

## What is a brag document?

A practice popularized by programmer and writer Julia Evans in 2019, now common across software teams: a personal document where you log what you did, the positive feedback you received, and the measurable impact of your work, on a regular cadence. You use it as source material for performance reviews, promotion packets, and resume updates. The point is simple — stop your brain from failing you when you try to recall a project you finished six months ago, right as you're filling out a review form.

Evans' own template splits the document into four sections: things you did, positive feedback you got, career goals, and a general notes section. You don't have to share it with anyone — you keep it for yourself first, then pull from it when writing a review or promotion case.

## Why shouldn't you wait until review day?

Because human memory overweights recent events and underweights old ones. This "recency bias" means that when you fill out a review form from memory, the last month or two dominates and a major project you finished in January gets skipped entirely. Evans says she updates her own document twice a year, in a few-hour session; some of her readers add short notes every two weeks instead. Whichever cadence you pick, logging a win the moment it happens beats trying to recall it months later — because right then, you still remember which metric moved and how many people it affected.

## How does recency bias distort a review?

HR-tech company Lattice names "recency bias" as one of the hardest cognitive biases for managers to shake: people weigh recent observations far more heavily than older ones when drawing a conclusion. The practical effect is simple and annoying — annual review cycles end up rewarding whoever finished the year strong, which pushes some employees to save their best work for the last quarter on purpose. Lattice's own suggested fix is exactly what a brag document does: self-reviews, mid-year check-ins, and folding feedback from every project into one running record on a regular basis. In other words, a brag document isn't a nice-to-have — it's a concrete defense against a named, well-documented bias.

## What are the most common brag-document mistakes?

Three mistakes show up repeatedly. First, entries that are too vague: "improved the backend" tells you nothing six months later — if you don't write down which system and which number changed at the time, you'll be guessing afterward. Second, reserving the document only for "big" wins: small but repeated collaboration contributions (mentoring a junior engineer, helping during an incident) look minor individually, but together they're exactly what fills the "independence" and "scope" sections of a promotion packet. Third, opening the document once and never updating it again — this is the single most common way to make a brag document worthless, because its entire value comes from small, regular additions, not one end-of-year memory-reconstruction session.

## What exactly goes in a brag document?

A plain "things I did" list isn't enough. Logging in four categories works better: concrete impact (which metric moved, how many users it touched), scope (how many teams or systems it involved), collaboration (who you worked with or mentored), and outside feedback (a manager's message, a customer's thank-you). Writing "fixed the checkout cache bug, page load dropped from 2.1s to 0.6s" does more work for both your manager and a promotion committee than writing "improved checkout."

| Category | Example entry |
|---|---|
| Concrete impact | "Fixed the checkout cache bug, load time 2.1s → 0.6s" |
| Scope | "Rewrote the auth library used by three teams" |
| Collaboration | "Mentored two junior engineers through code review" |
| Outside feedback | "PM cited this work as an example in sprint review" |

## How does a brag document become a promotion packet?

Most companies ask promotion packets to cover headings like "impact," "scope," and "independence" — which map almost directly onto the four categories above. During a promotion cycle, the only work left is to pick the five or six strongest entries from your log and rewrite them to fit your company's template; you're curating and editing, not recalling from scratch. The same entries double as material for self-reviews and LinkedIn updates. Our [guide to writing a self-review with AI](/en/posts/write-self-review-brag-doc-with-ai) covers how to turn these raw notes into review-ready prose with a language model.

A simple structure you can reuse as a template:

```text
## 2026 Q4
- [Impact] What did you do? What number moved?
- [Scope] How many people/teams/systems were affected?
- [Collaboration] Who did you work with or help?
- [Outside feedback] Who said what, and when? (quote + date)
```

## Should you share it with your manager?

It's not required, but it helps — especially when your manager changes, since a new manager has no memory of your past work at all. Sharing the entries from the current period in a 1:1, rather than the whole document, hands your manager the concrete material they need to advocate for you. Promotion packets usually require at least one peer review too; you can send that peer the same curated entries.

My own take: the real cost of skipping a brag document doesn't show up while you're filling out a review form — it shows up before the promotion conversation even happens. If your manager is deciding whether to nominate you and has no current, concrete file to work from, the decision gets made on "what I remember," and what people remember best is last month. A brag document doesn't eliminate that bias, but it at least makes sure the decision gets made with data in front of it.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### How often should I update a brag document?

There's no fixed rule — Julia Evans updates hers twice a year in one longer session, while some readers add short notes every two weeks. What matters is logging a win as it happens, or at least by month's end, instead of waiting until year-end and trying to reconstruct it from memory.

### Is a brag document the same as a promotion packet?

No. A brag document is the raw, chronological record you keep for yourself; a promotion packet is the final document where you select entries from that record and organize them to match your company's required format, usually under headings like impact, scope, and independence.

### I never kept one and my review is next week — what now?

Start now: scan your calendar for past meetings, search Slack and email for phrases like "thanks" or "great work," and comb through closed tickets and shipped projects to build a retroactive list. It won't be complete, but it beats starting from nothing — and keep logging going forward.

### Do I have to share my brag document with my manager?

No, it's optional. But sharing a few entries from the current period in 1:1s — especially around a manager change — gives your manager the concrete material they need to make your case in a review or promotion discussion.
