Short answer: in 2026, what decides a term sheet isn't the slides — it's seven "off-deck" areas: financial hygiene, founder-market fit, digital reputation and AI visibility, uncurated customer validation, technical scalability, and clean cap table hygiene. Founders who prepare these areas ahead of time are 3x more likely to land a term sheet than those who don't.

## Why is the pitch deck just an entry ticket now?

Short answer: because every founder can produce a polished deck now — AI tools erased that differentiator. So investors decide based on verifiable signals behind the deck instead: do the books match the slide, does the product actually work, is the founder actually positioned to solve this problem.

The seven areas, in order: financial hygiene and unit economics, visual brand consistency, founder-market fit, digital reputation and AI visibility, uncurated customer validation, technical scalability, and clean cap table hygiene. Founders who address all seven ahead of time are 3x more likely to secure a term sheet.

## How do investors check financial hygiene?

Short answer: investors measure the gap between your management accounts and your audited accounts; a gap exceeding 12% typically triggers an intensive audit. A mismatch between the growth chart in your deck and your actual books is one of the most common red flags investors find.

On unit economics, what actually gets scrutinized is customer acquisition cost (CAC) payback period and cohort-level retention — not a single "average churn" number, but an ARR/MRR bridge reconciliation showing exactly which cohort is dropping and why. Preparing this document ahead of time is one of the biggest time-savers in the diligence process.

## Why does cap table and IP hygiene matter this much?

Short answer: an unsigned IP assignment agreement is serious enough, on its own, to stall a round. If it turns out a founder or an early contractor never formally assigned code ownership to the company, the investor has to question who legally owns the company's core asset — the code itself.

The four most common red flags are: unsigned IP assignment agreements, inconsistencies between the deck's financials and the actual books, missing board consents for major decisions, and customer concentration above 30% (a single customer accounting for more than a third of revenue). These four are the cheapest items to self-audit before you ever enter full diligence.

## How do investors assess founder-market fit and brand consistency?

Short answer: investors look for concrete evidence of why this specific founder can solve this specific problem better than anyone else — prior industry experience, a lesson learned from an earlier failure, or having personally lived the problem. A generic "passionate founder" narrative no longer clears the bar; the question is more specific: why does this founder actually know this market better than everyone else?

Visual brand consistency looks minor at first glance, but it's read as an indirect signal of operational discipline: an inconsistent brand voice across the website, the product UI, the sales deck, and social accounts can raise the question of how much attention to detail the team actually has. It's rarely enough to sink a round on its own, but combined with other red flags, it accelerates a loss of confidence.

## What does digital reputation and AI visibility mean?

Short answer: investors now ask tools like ChatGPT or Perplexity about your company and check what comes back — the same thing your prospective customers are doing. If the AI-generated answer about your company is outdated, wrong, or empty, that's not just a marketing problem — it becomes a trust problem during diligence.

This intersects directly with staying visible in [an era where AI Overviews are eating clicks](/en/posts/ai-overviews-eating-clicks-survival): producing current, accurate, citable content is no longer just a marketing metric — it's a fundraising-prep item.

## How are uncurated customer validation and technical scalability tested?

Short answer: investors go beyond the reference customers you handpicked, checking independent reviews, organic social mentions, and customers they cold-call directly — uncurated validation carries far more weight than the "happy customer" quotes in your deck.

On the technical side, institutional buyers can now request 174 specific documents across ten categories, including concrete technical signals like test coverage, deployment frequency, incident response time, and open-source license audits. Having these ready before the round starts turns diligence into a matter of days instead of weeks.

| Area | What gets checked | Most common red flag |
|---|---|---|
| Financial hygiene | Gap between management accounts and audited accounts | Deviation exceeding 12% |
| Cap table / IP | Equity distribution, IP assignment agreements | Unsigned IP assignment |
| Digital reputation | Accuracy and visibility in AI tools | Outdated or wrong AI answer |
| Customer validation | Independent reviews and references | Curated quotes only |
| Technical scalability | Test coverage, deployment frequency, incident response | Missing documentation |

## How do you plan pre-round readiness?

Short answer: before diligence starts, apply the table above to your own company and fill every gap — especially items that take time to gather retroactively, like IP assignment agreements and board consents. Revisiting your [co-founder equity and vesting setup](/en/posts/cofounder-equity-vesting-explained) at this stage also means you won't get caught unprepared on cap table questions.

A practical order looks like this: tackle cap table and IP assignment agreements first, since they're the hardest items to gather retroactively; then reconcile your financial reports against audited accounts; and only then move to relatively fast fixes like digital reputation and customer references. Front-loading the most time-consuming work this way shortens your total prep time.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### What do investors check beyond the pitch deck?

They check seven areas: financial hygiene, visual brand consistency, founder-market fit, digital reputation and AI visibility, uncurated customer validation, technical scalability, and cap table hygiene. Founders who prepare these areas ahead of time are 3x more likely to secure a term sheet.

### What's the most common due diligence red flag?

Unsigned IP assignment agreements are among the most serious, because they call into question who legally owns the company's core asset. Inconsistencies between deck financials and actual books, and customer concentration above 30%, are the other two most common issues.

### What gap in financial audits intensifies diligence?

When the gap between management accounts and audited accounts exceeds 12%, investors typically move into a more intensive financial audit process. A consistent bookkeeping system that stays under that threshold directly affects how fast the process moves.

### Why has AI visibility become a diligence item?

Investors now ask tools like ChatGPT or Perplexity about your company and check the answer, because that's exactly what your prospective customers are doing too. An outdated or incorrect AI answer signals a problem on both the marketing and credibility fronts.
