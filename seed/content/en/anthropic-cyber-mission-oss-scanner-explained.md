Short answer: the Cyber Mission is Anthropic's October 8, 2026 security initiative with two parts — a Critical Infrastructure Defense Program (CIDP) that puts frontier Claude models in front of power grids, water systems, and transportation operators through 11 security vendors, and a free, opt-in OSS Scanner that emails open-source maintainers bug reports with a proof of concept and a suggested fix.

## What is Anthropic's Cyber Mission?

It's a long-term security program, announced October 8, 2026, built around two tracks aimed at infrastructure that's hard to patch and software that's maintained by volunteers. The first track, the Critical Infrastructure Defense Program, works through managed security providers rather than contacting plant operators directly — Anthropic gives those providers frontier Claude access, on-site engineers, and its own threat research. The second track, OSS Scanner, is a free vulnerability-scanning service for open-source projects.

The framing matters: Anthropic isn't claiming either track makes infrastructure or open-source code immune to attack. It's betting that giving defenders the same model capability attackers already have access to narrows a gap that's been widening since generative models got good at reading unfamiliar codebases fast.

## What does the free OSS Scanner actually do?

It scans an enrolled open-source repository and sends maintainers a report containing the vulnerability, a working proof of concept, and a suggested fix — generated by one of Anthropic's strongest models, with no human review before the report reaches the maintainer's inbox. Anthropic's own estimate puts the scanner's true-positive rate above 90%, a figure the company has published but that hasn't been independently audited yet, so treat it as a vendor claim rather than a settled benchmark.

That lack of human review is also the catch. Some reports will have the wrong severity rating or miss context only a maintainer would know, which is why Anthropic caps its liability for the service at $1,000 — a number worth knowing before you wire the scanner into anything you'd call a security process rather than a second opinion. Maintainers of qualifying projects can register at Anthropic's security portal; the service itself costs nothing.

## Who are the 11 founding partners, and what do they do?

The Critical Infrastructure Defense Program launched with Accenture, Booz Allen, CrowdStrike, Deloitte, Dragos, Hitachi, Insane Cyber, Nozomi Networks, Palo Alto Networks, PwC, and Rockwell Automation as founding partners. These are the vendors operators of power grids, water systems, and transportation networks already buy security services from — Anthropic is routing frontier-model access through that existing relationship instead of asking utilities to onboard a new AI vendor directly.

| Cyber Mission track | Who it's for | What you get | Cost |
|---|---|---|---|
| Critical Infrastructure Defense Program | Power, water, transport, government system operators (via a partner vendor) | Frontier Claude access, on-site Anthropic engineers, shared threat research | Through partner contracts, not public pricing |
| OSS Scanner | Maintainers of enrolled open-source projects | Automated vulnerability reports with PoC and suggested fix | Free |

## Why now — what problem is this actually solving?

Anthropic's own account of its earlier security work, Project Glasswing, is the real motivation: that program found plenty of bugs without meaningfully cutting cyber risk, because finding flaws with a capable model turned out to be the easy part. Triaging which findings matter, ranking them by real-world exploitability, and actually shipping fixes stayed slow and mostly manual. The Cyber Mission is a second attempt at the same problem — OSS Scanner tries to shrink the triage gap by shipping a fix suggestion alongside every report, and CIDP tries to put engineering capacity, not just model output, next to defenders who are chronically understaffed.

That gap is also why [AI slop is breaking open-source security](/en/posts/ai-slop-open-source-security): low-quality AI-generated pull requests and bug reports already overload maintainer inboxes, and a scanner that sends findings straight through without human review only works if its signal-to-noise ratio holds up at scale. The 90%+ true-positive claim is the number to watch as more projects enroll.

```text
A typical OSS Scanner report structure:
  - Vulnerability class and affected file/function
  - Proof-of-concept trigger (input or sequence that reproduces it)
  - Suggested fix (diff or patch outline)
  - Confidence/severity estimate (unaudited, may be wrong)
  - No human reviewer sign-off before delivery
```

## Is this different from the Cyber Verification Program?

Yes — they solve different problems. [Anthropic's Cyber Verification Program](/en/posts/what-is-anthropic-cyber-verification-program), expanded October 6, 2026, loosens usage restrictions for vetted security professionals who need Claude to behave more like an offensive tool during authorized testing. The Cyber Mission, announced two days later, is defense-side infrastructure: it doesn't change what Claude will do for a verified user, it adds a scanning service and a partner network. A security researcher might use both — verification to run an authorized penetration test, Cyber Mission's threat research to inform what they're testing for.

It also sits next to Anthropic's broader security disclosures, including its [2026 threat intelligence report](/en/posts/anthropic-2026-threat-intelligence-report) on how attackers are already using Claude-class models — context that makes the defensive framing of Cyber Mission read less like marketing and more like a direct response to documented misuse.

## Should your open-source project enroll in OSS Scanner?

Enroll if you maintain a project with real users and limited time to triage security reports yourself — a free automated first pass, even an imperfect one, is better than nothing for a project that currently gets zero security scanning. Don't treat it as a replacement for [SBOM and SLSA-level supply-chain practices](/en/posts/software-supply-chain-security-sbom-slsa): OSS Scanner finds bugs in your code, it doesn't verify your build provenance or your dependency chain. Read every report before merging a suggested fix — the $1,000 liability cap is Anthropic's own signal that you're the last line of review.

My take: the $1,000 cap is the most honest part of this announcement. It tells you exactly how much confidence Anthropic itself has in unreviewed model output reaching your inbox — not zero, but not "ship this fix without reading it" either.
