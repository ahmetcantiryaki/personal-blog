Short answer: push monthly in an early-stage SaaS because it lowers the friction to saying yes and gives you a fast feedback loop while you're still testing product-market fit; push annual once your customer base has stabilized, with a discount in the 10-20% range, because annual customers churn noticeably less than monthly customers and you get cash upfront. The right default depends on which problem your company is actually solving right now: cash and retention at scale, or conversion rate early on.

## Does an annual plan actually reduce churn?

Short answer: yes, and the gap isn't small — commonly cited industry figures put monthly churn on annual plans at roughly half to a third of what monthly plans see. The mechanism is simple: an annual customer doesn't get a monthly opportunity to reconsider their subscription, which compresses the "should I cancel this month" decision down to once a year instead of twelve times.

That doesn't mean annual plans magically increase satisfaction — it just means the *opportunity* to cancel shrinks. A dissatisfied annual customer can still leave in bulk the moment their renewal date arrives, so comparing annual churn directly to monthly churn can be misleading if you don't also account for renewal-date cliffs.

## How big should the annual discount actually be?

Short answer: according to Recurly's benchmark study of over 1,000 SaaS businesses, the single most common annual discount is 16.7% (equivalent to two months free), with 8.3% (one month free) as the second most common tier. The typical practical range is 10-20% off the amortized monthly price.

The same study found that lower-priced plans (under $10/month) tend to have higher churn, and a larger annual discount is a common retention tactic specifically in that segment. So treating your discount as a single fixed rule across every price tier is less effective than scaling it to your pricing segment.

| Plan strategy | Cash flow | Churn | Sales friction | Best-fit stage |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Monthly only | Weak (collected monthly) | High | Low (low commitment) | Early stage, still seeking product-market fit |
| Annual only | Strong (collected upfront) | Low | High (asks for a big commitment) | High ARPC, enterprise-leaning |
| Both, annual pushed | Moderate-strong | Moderate-low | Moderate | Growth stage (most SaaS) |

Per Recurly's data, 68.8% of SaaS businesses offer both plans; 27.0% offer monthly only, and 4.2% offer annual only. Companies with higher average revenue per customer (ARPC) were more likely to offer annual-only subscriptions.

## What's the hidden cost of an annual plan?

Short answer: the biggest hidden cost is a longer CAC (customer acquisition cost) payback period combined with higher refund exposure — a customer who prepays $1,200 and becomes unhappy three months in can demand a full or partial refund of the remaining term, while a monthly customer who churns has only lost you that one month. That's a real risk specifically for early-stage companies whose customer base hasn't stabilized yet, even if cash isn't tight.

When calculating CAC payback period (the metric showing how long it takes to recoup acquisition cost), it's misleading to treat an annual plan's upfront revenue as "paid back immediately" without first confirming that churn risk stays low across the actual commitment period — a refund months into the term erases that apparent payback.

There's a second hidden cost: support load. A customer who paid annually but stopped using the product after three months still looks "active" in your billing system, unlike a monthly customer, because their contract is still running — which means you need to track actual in-product usage separately from billing status. A team that only looks at billing data can easily mistake an abandoned account for a healthy one.

## Which company stage should push which plan?

Short answer: early-stage companies still searching for product-market fit should push monthly, because low commitment drives faster conversion and faster feedback; companies with growing MRR (monthly recurring revenue) that are focused on reducing churn should push annual, because it strengthens both cash flow and retention. Companies moving into enterprise sales typically shift to annual-only (or even multi-year) contracts, because enterprise buying processes are already long and budget cycles run annually anyway.

In practice, most successful SaaS companies offer both and visually push annual on the pricing page (e.g., with a "2 months free" badge) — a soft nudge toward annual without removing the monthly option entirely.

## How do you offer both without confusing buyers?

Short answer: make annual the default view on your pricing page but keep monthly one click away — rather than forcing users toward annual, show the exact savings in a concrete number ("save $240/year" rather than a vague "discounted"). Showing both plans side by side with the identical feature list prevents users from suspecting they're being steered into something.

My own take: most early-stage founders push annual too early because the cash flow looks appealing — but locking an unsolved churn problem into an annual contract doesn't fix the problem, it just defers it to the renewal date. Get your monthly churn to an acceptable level first, then use annual as a growth lever, not a patch.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### How big should an annual plan discount be?

According to Recurly's SaaS subscription plans benchmark study, the most common discount is 16.7% (two months free), with 8.3% (one month free) as the second most common tier; the typical practical range is 10-20% off the monthly price. A larger discount on lower-priced plans is a common tactic to reduce churn specifically in that segment.

### How much less do annual plans churn compared to monthly plans?

Commonly cited industry figures put monthly churn on annual plans at roughly half to a third of what monthly plans see. Part of that gap can come from dissatisfied customers deferring their cancellation to the renewal date rather than leaving mid-term, so the comparison isn't purely about satisfaction.

### Which plan should a new SaaS default to?

Push monthly while you're still searching for product-market fit, since low commitment lowers friction and speeds up feedback. Shift toward pushing annual once your MRR is growing and you're focused on reducing churn.

### Does offering both monthly and annual plans confuse customers?

Not if done correctly — per Recurly's data, 68.8% of SaaS companies already offer both plans. To avoid confusion, show the exact annual savings as a concrete number and present both plans side by side with an identical feature list.
