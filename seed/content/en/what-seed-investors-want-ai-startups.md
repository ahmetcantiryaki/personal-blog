Short answer: a slick demo isn't enough anymore. In 2026, investors look first at gross margin — 50–60% is treated as normal for an AI startup, and margin as high as 95% can itself raise the suspicion that you're "not actually an AI startup" but a thin wrapper — then at retention, then at a defensive line that doesn't depend on the underlying model.

## Why is "wrapper" such a dangerous label?

Wrapper means a startup's only real differentiation is a thin interface layered on top of a foundation model — the moment the model provider changes its price or quality, the company has nothing left. More than 50% of active AI VCs now cite data quality and exclusivity as their primary moat signal; "our model is better" isn't a moat because models improve fast. "Our customers' data accumulates in our platform and the product gets smarter with every interaction" counts as one. The difference is whether you're betting on the model itself or on the ownership you've built around it.

## What gross margin do investors now consider normal?

AI-first startups in 2026 run 50–60% gross margins on average, versus 80–90% for traditional SaaS. That gap is now the first thing investors pull on at seed. 35–55% is considered normal in year one, and 60%+ is considered strong. The counterintuitive part: margin as high as 95% can itself be a red flag, because it usually means the company isn't fully passing through its inference costs to customers, or isn't actually a deep AI product but a thin interface.

## Which metrics do investors actually probe?

The order runs like this:

1. **Retention**: the question investors ask most often now isn't "is it growing" but "can it hold users." The market has produced plenty of AI products with explosive growth that declined just as fast.
2. **Gross margin after inference costs**: not raw revenue, but what's left after model API costs are subtracted.
3. **Usage growth**: the real usage-volume curve over time, not seats sold.
4. **Revenue quality**: a company with $5M in revenue from pilots or consulting often raises capital with more difficulty than one with $500K in recurring revenue backed by strong retention and healthy margins.

## Where does defensibility come from once the model gets commoditized?

Model quality is being commoditized fast — this year's best model can be average six months later. That's why investors look for the moat outside the model itself: a proprietary data loop (does every customer interaction make the product smarter?), a distribution advantage (is reaching the customer cheaper than it is for competitors?), and workflow depth (how embedded is the product in the customer's daily operations, and how expensive would it be to rip out?). Without at least one of these, the company has little left once the model provider changes its price or ships a competing feature.

## Why does the team get so much weight at seed?

Founding-team career expertise remains the most heavily weighted factor at seed and Series A; investors look for founders who pair technical depth with domain expertise. Teams with backgrounds at frontier AI labs often command a "talent premium" — meaning a startup pitching the same idea with a less credentialed team will typically find fundraising harder.

| Signal | What investors want to see | Red flag |
|---|---|---|
| Gross margin | 50–60% (year 1: 35–55%) | 95%+ (suspicion of "not a real AI company") |
| Retention | Stable or rising usage over time | Explosive growth followed by a fast decline |
| Moat | Data loop, distribution, workflow depth | Only "our model is good" |
| Revenue | Small but recurring, high retention | Large but pilot- or consulting-heavy |

## What should you show at seed?

The strongest seed pitch shows the cleanest signal, not the biggest revenue number: a retention curve proving real users come back, positive unit economics after inference costs, and a moat you can describe clearly and that doesn't depend on the model itself. Traction is now slide two — before problem and solution — because investors are first answering the question "is this real."

## What slide order actually works?

The classic order — problem, solution, market, traction — is inverting in 2026. Investors want to see traction first, because the answer to "is this real" determines how much credit the rest of the story gets. A practical order: (1) the retention curve, (2) unit economics after inference costs, (3) the moat, made concrete as a data loop or distribution advantage, and only then (4) the problem/solution narrative and market size. Leading with the evidence investors trust most gives the rest of the pitch more credibility.

## What if margin is low but growth is fast?

Fast growth on thin margin at an early stage isn't disqualifying by itself — the real question is where the margin is heading over time. Investors look for a concrete plan to bring inference costs down: switching to a cheaper model, cutting repeated calls with caching, or benefiting from economies of scale as usage grows. If you have a clear answer for why margin is currently low, and that answer shows it's a temporary phase, low margin can be an acceptable starting point; without that answer, the same number becomes a red flag.

## The take

A slick demo may now do more harm than good. A demo shows what the product can do; margin and retention show whether the company can actually survive. A founder who spends an extra month polishing the demo while neglecting the margin table is polishing the wrong slide. The same discipline applies when deciding [bootstrap or VC](/en/posts/bootstrap-or-vc-2026): scaling a business before its unit economics are clear just scales the losses. Early clarity on fundamentals like [co-founder equity and vesting](/en/posts/cofounder-equity-vesting-explained) matters for the same reason — investors are evaluating the institution, not just the product.
