Short answer: dwell time (how long someone lingers on a post) and completion rate (what share of a video they finish) now carry more ranking weight than likes across Instagram, TikTok, YouTube, and LinkedIn. Both measure attention directly instead of inferring interest from a tap, which is why they're harder to game and increasingly decide what gets recommended.

## Why did platforms move from likes to attention signals?

Because the follower graph stopped being a reliable distribution mechanism once feeds shifted to interest-based recommendation — content now has to earn distribution on its own merits, regardless of how many followers the account has. A like takes one second and can be driven by habit, reciprocity, or a thumbnail alone; it tells a recommendation system almost nothing about whether the content actually held someone's attention.

Dwell time and completion rate don't have that problem. They're a direct read of behavior — did this person keep watching, keep scrolling back, keep reading — rather than a proxy that's one tap removed from the content itself. A platform optimizing for session-time and return visits has a straightforward incentive to weight the metric that predicts both.

## What is dwell time, and where is it most documented?

Dwell time is how long a viewer's attention stays on a specific piece of content before they move on — a pause on a photo, a lingering scroll-stop, or time spent reading a caption. It's most clearly documented on LinkedIn, where the platform's own engineering communications describe dwell time as an implicit feed-ranking signal: a longer look counts as a quality signal even when the viewer never likes, comments, or shares the post.

That "even without engagement" part is the key mechanic. A post someone stops to read for 20 seconds and never touches can outrank a post that gets an instant, reflexive like — because the system reads sustained attention as a stronger interest signal than a one-tap action.

## What is completion rate, and why does it matter more on short video?

Completion rate is the share of viewers who watch a video all the way through, and on short-form platforms it's one of the clearest predictors of further distribution. TikTok pushes a large majority of all views through its For You feed, and multiple 2026 creator-economy analyses converge on a rough rule of thumb: a completion rate around 70% is where a clip tends to clear the bar for broader push — though that figure is a practitioner estimate synthesized from testing and community data, not a number TikTok has published itself.

The mechanic behind it: a platform testing a new upload against a small initial audience uses completion (and rewatches) as the early signal deciding whether to push it to a larger one. A video that loses most viewers in the first three seconds never gets that second push, regardless of how good the ending is.

| Signal | What it measures | Best documented on | Gameable by vanity tactics? |
|---|---|---|---|
| Likes | A single tap, reflexive | Everywhere (legacy metric) | Yes — bait works |
| Dwell time | Sustained attention without action | LinkedIn | Hard — requires genuine interest |
| Completion rate | % of video watched through | TikTok, Reels, Shorts | Hard — requires pacing, not tricks |
| Rewatches | Same viewer, repeat views | TikTok, Reels | Hard — signals replay value |

## How do you design a hook, pacing, and loop for completion instead of for a like?

Front-load the reason to keep watching into the first two to three seconds, pace cuts so no single shot overstays its value, and end on a beat that makes rewatching the first few seconds feel worthwhile — a loop, a reveal that reframes the opening, or a punchline that recontextualizes the hook. None of that is designed to earn a like; all of it is designed to prevent a scroll-away.

A loop-friendly edit looks different from a reach-for-the-like edit. If the last frame cuts cleanly back into the first frame's action, a viewer who finishes often keeps watching into a second loop without realizing it — which both completion rate and rewatch count reward, and a like button never captures.

## Where do you actually read these signals in native analytics?

On TikTok, check average watch time and the retention graph in Analytics, not just views or likes — the retention curve shows exactly where viewers drop off. On Instagram, the Reels insights panel surfaces average watch time and plays versus reach. On YouTube Studio, "Average view duration" and the audience-retention graph are the direct equivalents; Shorts has its own retention curve under the same tab. On LinkedIn, dwell time isn't exposed as a raw number in creator analytics, so the closest proxy is comparing impressions to engagement rate and treating a high-impression, low-engagement post as evidence of strong dwell without action.

## Which vanity metrics should you stop optimizing for?

Stop chasing raw like counts and follower growth as primary success metrics — neither one predicts whether the algorithm will show your next post to anyone new. The honest take: most creators still check likes first out of habit, because it's the number displayed most prominently, not because it's the number that decides their reach. Retention curve and completion rate are less satisfying to glance at, but they're the ones actually running the recommendation engine in 2026.
