Short answer: smart glasses hardware split into three tiers in 2026 — camera eyewear, tethered AR glasses, and standalone AR computers — and each tier made always-on cameras more ordinary. Consent tools, recording indicators, and social norms did not keep pace, so capturing people without their knowledge is now an etiquette failure, not a technical one.

## What are the three tiers of the smart glasses market?

At AWE 2026 (Augmented World Expo), the industry's hardware split cleanly into three tiers, according to [Next Reality's event coverage](https://virtual.reality.news/news/awe-2026-smart-glasses-split-into-three-tiers-as-privacy-concerns-grow/), and that split now maps more closely to privacy risk than to price point alone.

The first tier sits at $300–800 and covers audio- and camera-equipped glasses with no display — Meta Ray-Ban and Even Realities G1 are the clearest examples. The second tier runs $2,000–3,000 and adds a transparent waveguide display, usually paired with a separate compute puck; Xreal's Aura and Snap's Specs, shipping this fall at $2,195, fall here. The third tier covers standalone AR computers that fold the display, onboard compute, and camera into one device without a tethered accessory.

| Tier | Price range | Display | Example devices |
|---|---|---|---|
| Camera eyewear | $300–800 | None | Meta Ray-Ban, Even Realities G1 |
| Tethered AR glasses | $2,000–3,000 | Transparent waveguide | Xreal Aura, Snap Specs ($2,195) |
| Standalone AR computer | Premium tier | Integrated, untethered | High-end AR headsets/glasses |

Per [ABI Research's 2026 consumer technology trends report](https://www.abiresearch.com/blog/consumer-technology-trends-2026), annual shipments of no-display smart glasses are forecast to grow from 3.34 million units in 2025 to more than 32 million by 2030 — a 57.5% compound annual growth rate (CAGR). The report frames this as AI shifting the glasses' value proposition from entertainment toward contextual assistance and hands-free information access.

This tiering sits behind the Meta-versus-Android XR race we covered in [AI Smart Glasses 2026](/en/posts/ai-smart-glasses-2026-meta-vs-android-xr) — the same hardware acceleration, just viewed through a consent lens instead of a specs sheet.

## Why do always-on cameras break bystander consent?

Because a traditional camera announced the moment of capture, while a smart-glasses camera is a sensor worn on the face and ready to record at any time. When someone wearing camera eyewear turns to look at you, you have no reliable way to know whether you are being recorded. Bystander consent — the idea that people near a recording device deserve a say in whether they appear in it — breaks down exactly at that asymmetry: the wearer makes a unilateral choice, and the bystander never finds out.

This is not abstract. A January 2026 BBC investigation found creators using Meta Ray-Ban glasses to covertly film women, with one such video reaching 1.3 million views. A commentary from Oxford's Saïd Business School adds that Meta has reportedly prototyped glasses that record continuously rather than only on activation. My own read on this: once manufacturers choose "ready by default" over "off by default," consent stops being a user-behavior problem and becomes a deliberate product decision — one the industry has not had to answer for yet.

## Does the recording light actually protect bystanders, or is it theater?

In most situations, no — a small LED is easy to miss in bright light or a crowded room, and that is exactly the condition under which bystander consent matters most. Data protection authorities in Ireland and Italy have raised this concern since 2021, arguing the light alone is not a sufficient consent mechanism for people nearby. Worse, that light has reportedly been disabled with cheap hardware hacks on some devices.

The deeper problem is what the light is designed to do. It lets a manufacturer check a compliance box, but it does not create a real social norm. A real norm changes behavior without anyone having to ask a question first; the recording-light theory, by contrast, requires a bystander to notice the light, correctly interpret what it means, and then trust that interpretation — three points of failure stacked on top of each other, and reliability drops at each one.

This mirrors the notification problem we examined in [A Privacy Checklist for Your Smart Home](/en/posts/smart-home-privacy-checklist): a visible indicator is not the same thing as an actual consent process.

Researchers are starting to treat this as an engineering problem, not just a policy one. In March 2026, researchers at the [CISPA Helmholtz Center for Information Security](https://cispa.de/en/research/publications/213050-now-you-see-me-now-you-don-t-consent-driven-privacy-for-smart-glasses) published a proposal for a consent-driven hardware architecture that automatically blurs bystanders' faces at the moment of capture. It is one of the first concrete technical alternatives to a recording light, but no manufacturer has shipped it yet.

## How far behind is the law?

In the US, 11 states — including California, Florida, and Illinois — require all-party consent for audio recording, but no reported court case has yet tested those laws specifically against smart glasses. In the UK, the Data (Use and Access) Act received royal assent in June 2026 without adding any dedicated rule for wearable cameras. In the EU, the European Data Protection Board (EDPB) is expected to publish a report on the "social acceptability" of smart glasses in summer 2026.

That gap has pushed institutions to write their own rules instead of waiting for legislators. Over the course of 2026, the US Air Force restricted smart glasses for uniformed personnel, New York state courthouses banned them, DefCon barred them from its conference floor, and Royal Caribbean restricted them on its ships; a UK Comic Con organizer also banned recording glasses after attendee complaints. Venue policy, not statute, has become the real enforcement layer.

## What etiquette should wearers, hosts, and venues follow now?

Until the law catches up, all three parties need to set their own expectations: the wearer should be transparent about the device, the host should state a policy in advance, and the venue should be ready to enforce a physical rule when it matters.

```text
Smart Glasses Etiquette - Quick Checklist

FOR WEARERS:
- Remove or verbally disclose glasses before entering a private space
- Never treat "the light isn't on" as proof you are not recording
- Stop immediately if someone asks you to stop

FOR HOSTS:
- State your smart glasses policy in the invite or at the door
- Collect glasses before changing rooms, bathrooms, or private talks
- Get explicit consent before glasses are worn around children

FOR VENUES:
- Post the glasses policy visibly at entry points
- Require glasses to be checked in sensitive areas (fitting rooms, clinics)
- Train staff not to treat "the recording light is off" as a safety guarantee
```

What ties these three rules together is that none of them depend on better hardware — they depend on communication. The continuous-sensing wearables we covered in [Health Wearables 2026](/en/posts/health-wearables-2026-rings-glucose) face a related trust problem, but rings and glucose monitors collect the wearer's own data; what makes smart glasses different is that the recording almost always includes someone else.

The speed of this hardware push is easier to understand through the lens of our piece on [Why AI Gadgets Keep Flopping](/en/posts/why-ai-gadgets-keep-flopping): manufacturers are choosing to solve the sales problem first and the social-acceptance problem later, if at all.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Can the recording light on smart glasses be turned off?

Yes — reporting indicates that on some models, the LED indicator can be disabled with cheap hardware hacks, which undermines its role as a bystander consent signal. Data protection regulators in Ireland and Italy have flagged this weakness since 2021.

### Is secretly recording someone with smart glasses legal in the US?

It depends on the state: 11 US states, including California, Florida, and Illinois, require all-party consent for audio recording, making covert audio capture a potential crime there. Video recording sits in a greyer, state-by-state area that has not been specifically tested against smart glasses in court.

### Where are smart glasses currently banned?

During 2026, the US Air Force restricted smart glasses for uniformed personnel, New York state courthouses banned them, DefCon barred them from its conference, and Royal Caribbean restricted them aboard its ships; a UK Comic Con event also banned recording glasses after attendees complained. These are institutional policies, not national laws.

### Does the EU have specific privacy rules for smart glasses yet?

Not yet — there is no dedicated EU law for smart glasses as of October 2026, but the European Data Protection Board is expected to publish a report on their "social acceptability" in summer 2026. That report could become the first formal framework for applying GDPR to bystander recording.
