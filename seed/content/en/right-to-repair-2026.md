Short answer: no, not always. The EU's right-to-repair directive has applied since July 31, 2026, and more than 13 US states now have some form of right-to-repair law, but none of these laws clearly bans "parts pairing" — the software lock that ties a replacement part to the manufacturer's approval. The result: the part is for sale, the manual is public, and the device still might not work right without going through an authorized channel.

That's the most frustrating detail hiding behind the "repairable" label. Read the law and a phone, laptop, or appliance sounds fixable. In practice, some parts run in a degraded mode — or trigger persistent warnings — until the manufacturer's own system signs off. Here's where both the EU and US stand as of November 2026, and what that gap actually means for you.

## When Did the EU's Right-to-Repair Directive Take Effect?

The EU's Directive (EU) 2024/1799 has applied since July 31, 2026, the date by which member states were required to transpose it into national law and start enforcing it. "Required to" and "did" are not the same thing: as of October 22, 2026, Germany and Italy were among the only major markets that had actually transposed it. The European Commission sent formal-notice letters to 18 member states on September 25, 2026, for incomplete transposition of the directive and its companion, Delegated Directive 2026/74.

The scope is also narrower than it sounds. The directive doesn't create a blanket free-repair right for every product — it only covers items that already carry EU ecodesign repairability duties, such as smartphones, tablets, and certain large appliances. Delegated Directive 2026/74 added local space heaters to that list starting July 31, 2026, with e-bike and scooter batteries joining on February 18, 2027.

## What Loopholes Do Critics Point to in the EU's Directive?

The biggest gap is that the directive never defines a "reasonable price" for a repair. It forces manufacturers to supply parts and manuals, but sets no ceiling on what they can charge for either — refurbed's analysis argues this leaves room to price repairs out of reach even where the law technically applies. The second gap is intellectual property and software: some manufacturers can still block specific repairs on IP or software-lock grounds even when the directive nominally covers the device.

## Which US States Have a Right-to-Repair Law?

There's still no federal right-to-repair law in the US — it's entirely state by state. Colorado's HB24-1121, the "Consumer Right to Repair Digital Electronic Equipment" act, took effect January 1, 2026, and is widely described as the broadest electronics repair law in the country so far. Washington's HB 1483 took effect the same day for digital electronic products, while earlier laws in New York, Minnesota, California, and Oregon (enacted between 2023 and 2025) were already active. Texas's HB 2963 took effect September 1, 2026.

By July 2026, a law-firm analysis from Faegre Drinker counted roughly 13 states with some form of enacted right-to-repair statute. PIRG estimates that, with the January 1, 2026 effective dates in place, about one in four Americans — roughly 25.75% — now live in a state with an enforceable right-to-repair law.

## Does "Repairable on Paper" Mean Actually Repairable?

No, and this is the real sticking point. A law can force a manufacturer to sell parts and publish manuals without banning "parts pairing" — the software check that confirms a replacement part was authorized by the manufacturer's own system. When a part fails that check, it can run in a crippled state or throw a persistent warning, even if the part itself is genuine or legally purchased. Almost none of the current EU or US laws close this gap outright.

Apple's long-running parts-pairing practice on iPhones is the best-known example: swap a screen or battery, even an authentic one, and the device can flag it as unverified or disable features such as True Tone color matching or Face ID until it's paired through an authorized channel. John Deere's software locks on its tractors are the older, more contentious version of the same pattern on farm equipment. The law says "the part can be sold." The device's software says "but it won't fully work." Both can be true at once.

The practical arbiter here isn't the statute — it's iFixit's independent repairability scoring and teardown reporting. A device can carry "legally repairable" status on paper while iFixit's teardown shows exactly how much parts pairing gets in the way, which screws need proprietary tools, and how stubborn the adhesive really is. The short version: check the iFixit score before you buy, not just whether a law applies.

## Which Devices Can You Realistically Fix Yourself?

Some repairs are genuinely beginner-friendly; others aren't worth the risk. Battery and screen swaps on phones with strong iFixit scores are a reasonable first repair, especially once you understand [why your phone's battery degrades](/en/posts/why-phone-battery-degrades-slow-it) in the first place. RAM and SSD upgrades are still open on many laptops, and joystick drift fixes on game controllers are usually solvable with a cheap part and a short video tutorial.

Skip soldered or fully sealed components and anything that needs a manufacturer's proprietary pairing tool — amateur attempts there risk bricking the device entirely. The basic toolkit is short: a precision screwdriver set with the right bit sizes, suction cups and spudgers for prying, a heat gun for stubborn adhesive, and basic anti-static handling. Given [why gadgets cost more in 2026](/en/posts/why-gadgets-cost-more-2026-memory), a successful small repair now saves real money.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### What does the EU's right-to-repair directive actually cover?

The EU's Directive (EU) 2024/1799 isn't a blanket repair right — it only covers products that already carry EU ecodesign repairability duties, such as smartphones, tablets, and certain large appliances, plus local space heaters added from July 31, 2026. E-bike and scooter batteries join the list on February 18, 2027.

### Which US states currently have a right-to-repair law in effect?

Colorado's and Washington's laws took effect January 1, 2026, while New York, Minnesota, California, and Oregon already had earlier laws active. Texas's law took effect September 1, 2026. By July 2026, roughly 13 states had enacted some form of right-to-repair statute, but there is still no federal law.

### What is parts pairing, and why does it undercut the right to repair?

Parts pairing is when a device's software checks whether a replacement part was authorized by the manufacturer's own system and shows a warning or disables features if it wasn't. Almost none of the current right-to-repair laws explicitly ban this practice, so a part can be legal to sell while the device still won't run it at full function.

### How do I know if a device is actually repairable before I buy it?

Check iFixit's independent repairability score and teardown report rather than relying on a law applying to the device category. It shows the real extent of parts pairing, which screws need proprietary tools, and how hard the adhesive is to work with — details a "legally repairable" label doesn't capture on its own.
