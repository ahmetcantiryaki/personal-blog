Short answer: no, not for most creators. Instagram raised the Reels recording and upload limit to 20 minutes, but its recommendation surface still prioritizes clips under 3 minutes for viewers who don't already follow you. Chasing length when your format doesn't need it just hurts discovery.

## When did Instagram raise the Reels limit to 20 minutes, and what changed?

Instagram [updated its Reels camera](https://petapixel.com/2025/11/25/instagram-updates-camera-so-you-can-film-20-minute-reels) in late 2025 (around November) to raise both the in-app recording limit and the camera-roll upload limit to 20 minutes. Meta described the change as giving creators "more creative freedom and room for longer storytelling." [According to BetaNews](https://betanews.com/article/meta-is-rolling-out-improvements-to-the-instagram-reels-camera), the cap had already climbed in steps — from 90 seconds, to 3 minutes, to 10 minutes — and 20 minutes is the latest rung on that ladder. The key distinction: this is a recording-and-upload ceiling, not a distribution guarantee. Instagram changed how long a Reel can be, not how it decides which Reels to recommend.

## Do long Reels get more views than short ones?

Short Reels still have the edge for reaching people who don't follow you. Instagram has said repeatedly that its recommendation surface prioritizes sub-3-minute content for non-follower discovery — that's a stated product choice, not a rumor. The math makes the gap obvious: an account that gets a 60% completion rate on a 3-minute Reel needs to hold attention five times longer to hit that same completion rate on a 15-minute Reel. For most accounts that's not a realistic bar, and a low completion rate tells the algorithm to show the video to fewer people next.

## Which formats actually suit a longer Reel, and which don't?

Longer runtime works when the viewer is already committed to staying: step-by-step tutorials, vlog-style day-in-the-life content, behind-the-scenes footage, extended interviews. These formats hold completion rate up because the viewer is watching to see what happens next. Trending audio, single-beat comedy, quick tips, and "hook then payoff" formats do the opposite when stretched — the viewer already got the payoff in the first 5 seconds, so the remaining 15 minutes just adds to the drop-off rate.

| Format type | Recommended length | Why |
|---|---|---|
| Quick tip / trending audio | 15–60 sec | The payoff is short; stretching it raises drop-off |
| Comedy / single beat | 30–90 sec | Viewers leave once the punchline lands |
| Product demo | 1–3 min | Staying short keeps it eligible for discovery ranking |
| Tutorial / step-by-step | 3–10 min | Viewers stay to see the result |
| Vlog / behind-the-scenes | 5–20 min | Targets intentional viewers (followers), not discovery |

## How do you keep viewers watching through a long Reel?

State in the first 3 seconds exactly what the viewer will learn or see by the end — that's the window where they decide whether to stay. For long videos, chaptering — on-screen markers like "Step 1," "Step 2," or spoken transition lines — helps the viewer track progress and nudges them to keep going "just a bit further." Dropping a small payoff every 60–90 seconds (new information, a visual change, a small reveal) keeps attention from decaying. One production note: a single unbroken camera angle across 15 minutes tires viewers out; cutting to a new angle or scene every few minutes resets their attention.

## How do you turn one long Reel into several short clips?

Instead of treating long-form and short-form as rivals, use the long Reel as raw material. Pull 3–5 standalone moments from a 15-minute tutorial — each one a self-contained "aha" moment or a clear result on its own — and publish them as separate 30–60 second Reels, each linking back to the full video. That approach generates discovery traffic through the short clips while routing genuinely interested viewers to the long one. One shoot turns into 4–6 shareable pieces instead of a single 15-minute video.

## Does the algorithm weigh completion rate or total watch time more?

Instagram's ranking system uses both, but they're different signals. Completion rate — the share of viewers who stay to the end — is easy to hit on a short video. Total watch time, summed in seconds across all viewers, can climb on a long video simply because the video itself is long. Instagram's stated priority is using high completion rate on short content as a discovery signal, which is why a 15-minute video doesn't rank in discovery just because it "produced more total watch time than a 3-minute video" — completion rate is still the gating threshold.

## So, should you go long?

My take is simple: don't use the 20-minute ceiling unless your format genuinely needs a long narrative arc. Tools like [Trial Reels, which let you test a hook before publishing](/en/posts/instagram-trial-reels-test-hooks-before-you-post), are the cheapest way to check whether the first 3 seconds of a video actually hold attention — and that test matters even more on a long video, because a failed hook means the remaining 19 minutes never get watched. ## Frequently Asked Questions

### What's the new Instagram Reels length limit?

As of November 2026, Instagram Reels accepts video up to 20 minutes long, for both in-app recording and camera-roll uploads. The limit came from a late-2025 update that replaced the previous 10-minute cap.

### Does posting a long Reel hurt my discovery reach?

It can, if the video can't hold a high completion rate among viewers who don't already follow you. Instagram's discovery surface prioritizes sub-3-minute content for non-followers, so a long video tends to work better for engaging existing followers than for generating new reach.

### Which content types actually benefit from 20-minute Reels?

Step-by-step tutorials, vlog-style day-in-the-life content, and extended behind-the-scenes footage benefit because viewers are already intending to stay for the result. Short formats like trending audio, comedy beats, and quick tips see higher drop-off when stretched to that length.

### Is it worth splitting a long Reel into shorter clips?

Yes. Pulling 3–5 standalone "aha" moments from a 15-minute video and publishing each as its own 30–60 second Reel is a practical way to generate discovery traffic through short clips while still directing interested viewers to the full-length version.
