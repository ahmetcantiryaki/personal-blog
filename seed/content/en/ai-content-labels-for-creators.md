Short answer: if you're posting digitally created or altered video or audio that looks photorealistic or sounds real, you're required to add an "AI info" label. Meta now detects this automatically, without needing you to declare anything, by reading C2PA metadata embedded in the file — skip the label and you risk a penalty.

## How does Meta's AI content labeling system work?

Meta applies an "AI info" label to content it detects as AI-generated, or that creators disclose themselves, across Facebook, Instagram, and Threads. Organic posts have carried a "Made with AI" label since May 2026, and on the advertising side, this labeling went fully automatic starting July 2026.

Detection relies on the "AI generated" information built into the C2PA and IPTC technical standards — tools from Google, OpenAI, Microsoft, Adobe, Midjourney, and Shutterstock embed this into the file. Third-party tools like Photoshop's generative fill, DALL-E, Firefly, and Canva trigger the same mechanism; Meta reads the C2PA Content Credentials these tools bake into a file and labels an ad the moment it's uploaded.

## What content actually has to be labeled?

Meta draws a clear line: creators posting organic content with photorealistic video or realistic-sounding audio that was digitally created or altered are required to use its disclosure and labeling tool. Meta states it plainly: it may apply penalties to those who fail to do so.

This threshold matters because not every AI assist falls under it — editing text with AI, lightly touching up a background, or cleaning noise out of audio doesn't trigger this requirement. What crosses the line is photorealism: whether a viewer would reasonably mistake the video or audio for something real.

| Situation | Label required? |
|---|---|
| Photorealistic AI video (mimicking a real event) | Yes |
| Realistic AI voice clone | Yes |
| Minor AI color/lighting correction | No |
| AI-generated image used in an ad (with C2PA metadata) | Yes, detected automatically |
| Text-based AI content (article, captions) | No (rule targets visual/audio) |

## Does labeling actually hurt reach?

No — at least, the data doesn't support that, and our take runs against the common fear here: honest disclosure done right doesn't break trust, it builds it. A viewer who learns upfront that content is AI-generated gives a brand more credit for transparency than one who finds out later and feels misled.

The real risk sits in skipping the label and getting caught later: an AI-generated "customer testimonial" video mistaken for real, once exposed, causes far more reputational damage than a single label ever would have. A label can feel like a "this isn't real" warning in the short term, but it protects an account's credibility capital over the long run.

## How does the detection mechanism (C2PA) actually work?

C2PA (Content Provenance and Authenticity) is an industry standard that embeds cryptographically signed metadata about how a file was created directly into the file itself. When an image or video is produced with DALL-E, Firefly, or a similar tool, that metadata gets attached automatically, and platforms like Meta can read it and label the content even if the user discloses nothing.

The practical consequence: trying to strip that metadata through simple tricks like screenshotting and re-uploading, or converting the file format, violates both platform rules and, for ads, legal disclosure obligations. Deliberately removing that metadata is treated as a more serious violation than simply skipping the label.

## A disclosure policy for creators and brands

A practical rule set works like this: first, determine whether the content includes photorealistic video or realistic audio; if it does, use the platform's built-in AI labeling tool rather than trying to strip metadata; if it doesn't (a simple edit, color correction), a label isn't required, but adding a transparency note still builds trust.

```text
Example disclosure line: "The product demo in this video was
AI-generated and is not real usage footage."
```

That one sentence satisfies the platform requirement and gives viewers a clear frame. For anyone wondering whether [AI UGC ads actually convert](/en/posts/ai-ugc-performance-ads), this kind of disclosure practice is a way to keep conversion rates intact without losing the label requirement.

## What's the real penalty for skipping a label?

Meta sums up its rule as "penalties may apply" for skipping disclosure, but what that actually means varies by case: repeated violations can get content's reach restricted, an account flagged as "low trust," or an ad account's review process slowed down. A one-off, good-faith miss usually results in a warning; the real risk is deliberately trying to strip the metadata or repeatedly posting without disclosure.

The practical check for an agency or brand should be: add a step to your content production process, before publishing, that asks "is this photorealistic, and was it AI-generated?" Putting that check at the start of the workflow instead of the end is cheaper than going back to add a label after the fact.

## Does every platform apply the same rule?

No — the threshold and enforcement vary by platform. Meta's focus is photorealistic video and realistic audio; YouTube requires its "altered or synthetic content" label under a broader scope, for content that convincingly simulates a real person or event. TikTok similarly requires an "AI-generated" label for AI content and uses a metadata-based detection mechanism as well.

For a creator posting across multiple platforms, the practical takeaway is to apply the strictest rule (usually mandatory disclosure for photorealistic video and realistic audio) across all of them, rather than tracking each platform's threshold separately — it's safer and takes less time.

## How do these rules affect advertisers?

On the ad side, labeling no longer depends on the advertiser's own declaration — Meta scans every uploaded ad for C2PA metadata and labels it automatically on a match. That voids the assumption that an agency could quietly run an AI-generated visual for a client "unnoticed" — if the metadata is there, the label is unavoidable.

Our take is that labeling should be framed as a quality signal, not a confession: a brand stating its AI use clearly and confidently tells viewers "we have nothing to hide." Shifting that framing from a defensive posture to proactive transparency pays off for brand credibility over time.
