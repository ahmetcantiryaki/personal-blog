Short answer: Anthropic filed its S-1 registration statement with the US Securities and Exchange Commission on October 26, 2026, to list on the Nasdaq Global Select Market under the ticker "ANT." The filing discloses $16.29 billion in revenue for the first half of 2026 (up 1,304% year over year) alongside an $80.7 billion net loss for the same period, and the company is reportedly targeting a $1.5–2 trillion IPO valuation, up from its $965 billion private-round valuation.

## What does Anthropic's S-1 actually disclose?

An S-1 is the registration statement a company must file with the SEC before a US stock exchange listing, and it discloses financials, risk factors, and governance structure to the public for the first time. Anthropic's filing appears on SEC EDGAR under "Anthropic, PBC" and shows full-year 2025 revenue of $4.59 billion, climbing to $16.29 billion in just the first six months of 2026. That compares to $1.16 billion in the same period a year earlier — roughly a fourteenfold jump in six months.

The loss side of the ledger is more nuanced. The reported $80.7 billion net loss for H1 2026 is dominated by a $72.4 billion non-cash accounting charge tied to the fair-value remeasurement of convertible notes — not cash leaving the business. This kind of charge is common in S-1 filings from companies that raised large convertible rounds, and it is routinely misread as an operating loss when it isn't one.

## What exchange and ticker will Anthropic trade under?

Anthropic plans to list on the Nasdaq Global Select Market under the ticker "ANT." Worth noting for anyone searching the symbol: a six-person, pre-revenue mining company called Anteros Metals already trades as "ANT" on the Canadian Securities Exchange — a different company on a different exchange, so confirm Nasdaq specifically when you look this up.

## How much is Anthropic trying to be valued at?

The company's last private valuation was $965 billion, set after a $65 billion Series H round that closed in May 2026. Reports on the IPO target range from $1.5 trillion to $2 trillion — roughly 1.5 to 2 times the last private mark. The exact price range and share count aren't disclosed in this version of the S-1; those typically surface in an S-1/A amendment filed days before the actual offering.

## Who's underwriting the deal, and when will it actually happen?

Goldman Sachs and Morgan Stanley lead the underwriting syndicate, with reports putting the total underwriter count between 20 and 24 — a syndicate that size signals how large the market expects this deal to be. Timing is still unsettled across sources: some reports point to the week of November 16, 2026, others to Thanksgiving week starting November 23. SEC review and roadshow length can shift either date, so the real timeline will firm up with S-1/A amendments.

But the real story in the S-1 is how revenue growth and a massive accounting loss can coexist: the capital intensity of training models and running inference at scale shows a company still prioritizing scale over near-term profit. ## How does Anthropic's Public Benefit Corporation structure affect the IPO?

Anthropic is structured as a Public Benefit Corporation (PBC), which legally requires its board to weigh a stated public-benefit and safety purpose alongside shareholder returns, not just shareholder returns alone. That creates a balance rarely seen in a publicly traded tech company: even if an activist investor pushes for short-term profit, the board remains bound by charter to its safety commitments. The S-1 also details a Long-Term Benefit Trust, a structure empowered to select certain board members specifically to keep the company's safety mission independent of shareholder pressure — physicist Christopher Stubbs joining that trust in October 2026 is a sign the structure is actively staffed, not just a document.

This governance structure also creates an unusual risk factor for public markets: the S-1's risk section states plainly that the board's safety-driven decisions could conflict with short-term shareholder returns. For investors, that means Anthropic stock can't be evaluated as a conventional growth stock — the company's charter obligations add a decision variable that an ordinary public company doesn't carry.

My own read: treating the $72.4 billion loss as a sign the company is in trouble would be a mistake — it's not a cash outflow, it's an accounting remeasurement of convertible notes required under US GAAP. The number actually worth tracking is whether that 1,304% year-over-year revenue growth rate holds up in the quarters after the IPO, when growth naturally slows from a larger base.
