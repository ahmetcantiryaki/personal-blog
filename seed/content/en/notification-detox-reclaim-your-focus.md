Short answer: audit every app that can ping you, cut anything that isn't a person or a genuine emergency, and batch the rest into two or three fixed windows a day. A focus app can't fix a notification problem — the fix is fewer inputs, not a smarter filter on top of the same flood.

## What does one notification actually cost you?

A single interruption costs roughly 23 minutes and 15 seconds of recovery time before you're back at your original level of focus, according to Gloria Mark's research tracking knowledge workers. Her data also shows people rarely return directly to the interrupted task — they typically drift through about two other tasks first, stretching the real cost well past that 23-minute figure.

The reason a "quick check" of a notification does more damage than the notification itself is what Sophie Leroy named attention residue in 2009: part of your cognitive attention keeps processing the interrupted task even after you've moved on, degrading performance on whatever you switch to next. Mark's more recent tracking found something related and separate — average attention span on a screen has dropped to about 47 seconds, down from roughly 2.5 minutes in 2004, as constant task-switching becomes the default rather than the exception.

## How do you audit your notifications?

Open your phone's notification settings and list every app with permission to interrupt you, then sort each one into exactly one of three tiers: people who need you in real time (calls, a manager, your kid's school), things that can wait an hour, and things that can wait a day or don't need a notification at all. Most people find their list is 80% tier two and three, alerting them the instant something happens that could have waited.

```text
Tier 1 — interrupts immediately: phone calls, direct messages from your manager,
family emergency contacts.
Tier 2 — batched hourly: Slack/Teams channels, work email, calendar reminders.
Tier 3 — batched once daily or muted entirely: social media, news apps, most
app badge counts, newsletter notifications.
```

Anything you can't confidently place in tier one gets demoted by default — the audit only works if you're stricter than feels comfortable on the first pass.

## How do you set up tiered channels and Do Not Disturb schedules?

Once you have your three tiers, configure them at the OS level instead of relying on willpower to ignore a badge. iOS Focus modes, Android's Digital Wellbeing and Do Not Disturb, and Windows/macOS Focus Assist all let you allow tier-one contacts through while silencing everything else, on a recurring schedule rather than something you have to remember to turn on each morning.

| Platform | Feature | What it silences on schedule |
|---|---|---|
| iOS | Focus modes | All apps except allowed contacts and apps |
| Android | Do Not Disturb + Digital Wellbeing | Notifications and calls except starred contacts |
| macOS / Windows | Focus Assist / Focus filters | App notifications except pinned priority apps |

Set a recurring "deep work" schedule — most people find two or three 90-minute blocks a day realistic — rather than one heroic all-day block you'll break by lunchtime.

## How do you batch Slack and email instead of checking constantly?

Turn off push notifications for email and chat entirely, and instead check them at three fixed points: once mid-morning, once after lunch, once before you close out the day. This directly attacks the attention-residue problem, because you're processing messages in one continuous block instead of fragmenting your day into dozens of tiny interrupted-and-resumed tasks.

The objection people raise here is "what if something urgent comes in between checks" — which is exactly what tier one is for. If a message genuinely can't wait 90 minutes, it should come through a channel that bypasses Do Not Disturb (a call, a starred contact), not a Slack ping sitting in the same inbox as a lunch-order poll.

Badge counts deserve the same treatment as push notifications, even though they feel passive. A red "47" sitting on an app icon triggers the same attention residue Leroy described — you know it's there, part of your attention keeps tracking it, and you end up checking well before your next scheduled window just to make the number disappear. Turning off badge counts for tier-two and tier-three apps removes that passive pull without touching the notification settings themselves.

## How do you measure the time you actually reclaim?

Track two numbers for two weeks: total notifications received per day, and how many uninterrupted 60+ minute blocks you completed. Most people cutting from 100+ daily notifications down to a tiered system land at 15–25 tier-one interruptions a day, and gain two to four uninterrupted blocks they didn't have before.

The genuinely contrarian part of this is that the productivity-app industry sells the opposite lesson: install a better dashboard, a smarter triage tool, a focus timer with more features. All of that is still processing the same volume of inputs, just with nicer UI around it. The interruption cost doesn't come from how you sort notifications — it comes from how many of them exist in the first place, and no app fixes that by adding more software to check.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### How long does it actually take to recover from a notification interruption?

Roughly 23 minutes and 15 seconds on average, based on Gloria Mark's tracking of knowledge workers, and people typically drift through about two other tasks before returning to the original one — so the practical cost is often higher than that number alone suggests.

### Do focus and productivity apps actually reduce interruption cost?

Not by themselves. They organize the same volume of notifications more attractively, but the cost comes from how many interruptions occur, not how they're displayed. Cutting the number of apps allowed to notify you does more than adding another app to manage the ones you already have.

### How many notification tiers should I set up?

Three is enough for most people: real-time (calls and true emergencies), hourly batches (work chat and email), and daily or muted (social media, news, most badge counts). More tiers than that usually just adds decision fatigue back into the system you're trying to simplify.

### What's the fastest way to start a notification detox today?

Open your phone's notification settings, turn off push alerts for every app that isn't a person messaging you directly, and set one Do Not Disturb schedule covering your first deep-work block tomorrow morning. That single change removes most of the daily interruption volume without needing a new app.
