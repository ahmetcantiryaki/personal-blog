Short answer: it's not becoming the norm. A small group of large employers, including Amazon, JPMorgan Chase, Home Depot, and AT&T, now require five office days a week, and that's driven the headlines. But across the Fortune 500 overall, fixed hybrid work is still the dominant model, and most companies haven't dropped flexibility entirely.

## Which Companies Actually Require Five Days in the Office?

Yes, a handful of large, established employers have moved to full five-day, in-office requirements. JPMorgan Chase's full-time, all-staff in-office mandate took effect in April 2025 and became a reference point for the trend that followed.

Amazon, Home Depot, and AT&T have made similar moves. What these companies share is scale, an established corporate structure, and roots in finance or retail. Younger, more distributed software companies have been far less likely to adopt mandates this strict.

| Company | Policy | Effective |
| --- | --- | --- |
| JPMorgan Chase | 5 days/week, all staff | April 2025 |
| Amazon | 5 days/week in-office | 2025 |
| Home Depot | 5 days/week (corporate staff) | 2025 |
| AT&T | 5 days/week in-office | 2025 |

These four companies made headlines because they're the exception, not the rule. [Savills' review of the top 200 Fortune 500 companies](https://fortune.com/2025/07/18/return-to-office-hybrid-work-fortune-100-companies/) found that fixed hybrid work remains the dominant workplace model overall, with only a modest year-over-year shift toward stricter in-office requirements.

## What Percentage of Companies Will Require Office Time in 2026?

A 2025 survey of roughly 1,000 US business leaders found that almost half of companies plan to require employees to be in the office at least four days a week in 2026, [as reported by BenefitsPro](https://www.benefitspro.com/2025/10/09/nearly-half-of-companies-to-require-4-office-days-in-2026/), citing the survey. That points to a real shift toward a "four-day floor," but it also means roughly the other half of companies aren't moving that direction.

At the same time, remote work data doesn't show a collapse. A workforce tracker found that 21.7% of US employees worked remotely at least partially in June 2026, down only slightly from 22.3% in June 2024. In other words, the share of people working remotely in some capacity barely moved even as mandate headlines piled up.

## Does Return-to-Office Actually Improve Productivity?

Short answer: the evidence doesn't clearly support that claim. A University of Pittsburgh study of S&P 500 companies found no significant change in financial performance or market value after RTO mandates took effect.

By contrast, a Baylor-affiliated study covering more than 3 million workers reported an average 13–14% increase in employee turnover after RTO mandates, as reported by Hubstaff/founderreports, citing that research. [Gartner's June 2026 research](https://www.gartner.com/en/documents/8072365) reaches a similar conclusion: more time in the office does not automatically improve productivity, and it may raise longer-term talent-retention risk.

| Finding | Source | Result |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Financial performance/market value | University of Pittsburgh, S&P 500 | No significant change |
| Employee turnover | Baylor-affiliated study, 3M+ workers | 13–14% increase |
| Productivity and retention risk | Gartner, June 2026 | No automatic productivity gain, retention risk rises |

Put these three findings side by side and the picture is clear: there's no solid evidence that RTO mandates make companies measurably better off financially, but there's real evidence they increase the risk of losing employees.

There's an important distinction here: most "productivity" measurement happens at the company level, looking at annual financial results. A large-scale, publicly available study measuring individual-level output, such as code shipped, meetings run, or project completion time, doesn't yet exist. That's part of why this debate stays so polarized: both sides can point to their own anecdotes, because the aggregate company-level data doesn't definitively settle either argument.

## Why Do Companies Push RTO Despite Mixed Data?

Short answer: it's more likely driven by management control and real estate costs than by productivity evidence. Companies are already locked into long-term office leases, and empty floors create both a cost problem and a visibility problem for executives who signed those leases.

There's also a management-layer factor at play. Middle managers often feel they need to physically see their teams to manage them well. Managing distributed teams requires different skills, and some managers who haven't made that shift treat a return-to-office mandate as a way to fall back on familiar, in-person oversight. That motivation may be legitimate on its own terms, but it shouldn't be confused with a productivity argument; they're separate justifications, even when companies blend them in public statements.

## What Does This Mean for Software Professionals?

If your employer is still hybrid, that's normal, and you're still in the majority. If your employer has moved to a five-day mandate, that decision is more likely driven by management control, culture, or real estate costs than by productivity data, because the evidence we have doesn't show these mandates increase output.

In practice, if you're job hunting, ask directly in the interview: "What's the current office policy for this role, and is it likely to change?" Verbal reassurances during hiring aren't as reliable as a written policy; some companies emphasize flexibility while recruiting and tighten the policy later, once you're already on staff.

## My Take: These Mandates Are About Control, Not Evidence

I'll say this plainly: based on the current evidence, it's hard to defend five-day office mandates on productivity grounds. The Pittsburgh data shows no financial gain, and the Baylor data shows higher turnover, which suggests many companies are making this call based on managerial visibility and sunk real estate costs, not output data. That doesn't mean mandates are always pointless; some teams genuinely benefit from in-person collaboration. But the "return to office, and productivity will follow" pitch doesn't match what the data actually shows.
