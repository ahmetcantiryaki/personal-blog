Short answer: a scroll-stopping opener contains a concrete number, unresolved tension, or a direct question tied to the viewer's own situation. For video, this "first line" is the first 3 seconds; for text, it's whatever the platform shows before truncating (around 140 characters on LinkedIn) — both need to earn interest entirely on their own, before anyone reads the rest.

## Why does the opening line decide a post's fate?

Because algorithms and humans read the same signal: early drop-off. In December 2025, Instagram added a "skip rate" metric to its Insights API — [as SocialSamosa reported](https://www.socialsamosa.com/news-2/instagram-retention-chart-skip-rate-new-performance-metrics-reels-9730992), it replaced the earlier "view rate" metric and measures the share of viewers who swipe away within the first 3 seconds of playback. The platform doesn't publish an official "good" threshold for it, but what it measures is clear: if content doesn't earn attention in those first seconds, the algorithm already deprioritizes the rest of the video before showing it to anyone else. Third-party analytics tools (not official platform data, just estimated benchmarks) put the average 3-second watch-through rate at 20–35%, call 35–50% "good," and anything above 50% "excellent" — these numbers vary by platform and niche and aren't an authoritative reference.

## Which opening patterns actually work?

Four patterns work repeatedly: specificity (opening with a number or a concrete detail — "in 3 months," "with 127 customers"), tension (an unfinished sentence, or a situation cut off by "but"), a direct question (tied to the viewer's own situation — "do you also..."), and a pattern break (an unexpected visual or phrase that makes the viewer think "wait, what?"). All four do the same job: they give the viewer a reason to believe "I'll learn something here" before reading or watching the rest.

| Pattern | Example opener |
|---|---|
| Specificity | "This one change raised our conversion rate 40%" |
| Tension | "I was doing this wrong for three months, then I noticed..." |
| Direct question | "Do you also feel your stomach drop opening your inbox every Monday?" |
| Pattern break | "I screenshotted this to show my boss" |

## Where do platforms actually cut off the first line?

On text platforms, the truncation point directly sets how long your hook needs to be. LinkedIn officially states a 3,000-character cap per post, but it doesn't publish where the "see more" link appears. The commonly observed figure — [and sources disagree here, as one character-limit roundup notes](https://contentin.io/glossary/character-limit/) — is around 140 characters on mobile and about 210 on desktop before truncation; treat these as observed estimates, not official numbers. The practical takeaway: whatever falls within the first 140 characters needs to stand on its own and earn curiosity, in case that's all anyone sees.

```text
The 140-character test:
Read only the post's first 140 characters on their own.
Without reading further, do you understand it? Are you curious?
If the answer is "no," rewrite the opener.
```

## How do you actually test a hook?

The most reliable method is holding the body of the content constant and changing only the opener. For video, that means shooting 3–4 different versions of just the first 3 seconds of the same clip and comparing which one raises average watch time; for text, it means posting the same update on different days with different opening lines and comparing which raises click-through or the "see more" expansion rate. If your sample is small (under roughly 1,000 views), drawing a firm conclusion from one test is risky — instead, repeat the same pattern over a few weeks and look for a consistent trend.

One thing to watch for when testing: comparing two openers without holding the day and time of posting constant produces misleading results, because audience composition shifts throughout the day. Where possible, testing on the same day across different accounts, or close together in time on the same account, minimizes that time-of-day effect.

## How is a hook different from a headline in written content?

For a blog post or newsletter, the "hook" actually lives in two separate places: the subject line or headline (which decides whether someone opens it) and the first paragraph (which decides whether they keep reading once it's open). They don't do the same job — a headline needs to earn attention without overselling, because an exaggerated headline can raise the open rate while costing reader trust if the content doesn't deliver on it. The first paragraph needs to immediately make good on the headline's promise; this is also the most critical section for AEO, since a language model or search engine largely infers what a page is about from its first paragraph.

## How does the hook change on sound-off platforms?

Most viewers on TikTok and Reels watch with sound off, which means the "first line" needs to land through the eyes, not the ears. Opening with only a spoken line and no large, readable text overlay in the first frame means missing the entire sound-off portion of your audience. My own take: most creators treat the hook as a line they write once and forget. The strongest accounts instead write 8–10 different openers for the same piece of content and test which one holds attention longer — the hook deserves more A/B testing than the content itself, because changing it costs nothing and its effect is disproportionately large.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Is losing viewers in the first 3 seconds normal?

Third-party analytics tools put the average 3-second drop-off between 20–35%; this isn't an official platform-published threshold, just an estimated benchmark. 35–50% is generally considered "good," though there's no fixed platform standard.

### Where does LinkedIn cut off a post?

LinkedIn's official post limit is 3,000 characters, but it doesn't publish where the "see more" link appears; the commonly observed estimate is around 140 characters on mobile and 210 on desktop — treat these as estimates, not official figures.

### How many different openers should I test per post?

There's no fixed number, but strong accounts typically write 8–10 different opening lines and test which one increases watch time or click-through; since the hook is short, this kind of testing is low-cost.

### How should hooks differ on sound-off platforms?

Instead of relying on a spoken opener, use a large, readable text overlay in the first frame — since most TikTok and Reels viewers watch with sound off, a hook that depends purely on voiceover misses that entire audience.
