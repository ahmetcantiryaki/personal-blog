Short answer: a weekly review stops dissolving by Tuesday once it's a fixed 25-minute time box instead of a vague intention. Same day, same time, every week: clear your inboxes, scan the calendar, pick three outcomes, park everything else. The tool doesn't matter — the discipline does.

If Monday starts with good intentions and Tuesday afternoon is already chaos, the problem isn't motivation. It's the missing ritual that resets the week.

## Why do you need a weekly review if you already have a daily to-do list?

A daily list only holds that day's tasks — it misses the bigger picture: a finished project, a forgotten promise, a decision you keep postponing all slip through a day-by-day list. David Allen's Getting Things Done (GTD) method built the weekly review specifically to close that gap, clearing your mind of "open loops" so you start the next week with a clean slate. [GTD's official 11-step review](https://gettingthingsdone.com/2025/01/11-steps-of-the-weekly-review/), even in its early-2025 refresh, still runs on the same three phases as the 2001 original: get clear, get current, get creative.

This guide skips the full 11 steps and uses a stripped-down version one person can finish solo in 25 minutes.

## How do you split a 25-minute time box?

Five minutes to clear inboxes, eight minutes to scan the calendar, seven minutes to pick three outcomes, five minutes to park the rest — 25 minutes total, one sitting, same day and time every week. Set a visible timer; when it runs out, move to the next step wherever you are, because there's no such thing as a perfect review, only a finished one.

| Step | Time | Output |
|---|---|---|
| Clear inboxes | 5 min | Email, notes app, and paper notes reset to zero |
| Scan the calendar | 8 min | Last week's misses and next week's conflicts flagged |
| Pick three outcomes | 7 min | Three concrete goals for the week, written down |
| Park the rest | 5 min | Everything else moved to a someday list, out of your head |

Friday afternoon and Sunday evening are the two least chaotic slots; a review done mid-week gets tangled up with work that isn't finished yet.

## How do you clear your inboxes?

Go through email, your notes app, and any physical notes one item at a time with a single decision for each: respond, delete, or move it to the someday list — you're not reading, you're deciding. The five-minute limit is deliberately tight: replying to a contentious email isn't this block's job, tagging it "answer by Thursday" is.

The point of this step isn't an empty inbox — it's giving every item a place to live. The two-minute rule from [Systems That Beat Procrastination](/en/posts/beat-procrastination-systems-that-stick) applies here too: finish anything under two minutes immediately, park anything longer.

## How do you review the calendar?

Look back at last week's calendar and note in three lines what got done, which meeting was wasted, and where you made a promise you didn't keep; then look ahead and flag next week's conflicts and any meeting that needs prep. This two-way scan is the single step that shows the real gap between how the week actually went and the plan you intended.

Planning only the future without looking back at the past is how the same mistake repeats every week; the backward glance is the most valuable part of those eight minutes.

## How do you pick the week's three outcomes?

Skip a ten-item to-do list and write three concrete outcomes you can call "done" by the end of the week; each one should fit in a single sentence with a measurable finish line. Not "move the marketing report forward" but "share the first draft of the November marketing report by Friday."

Picking more than three turns the weekly review back into a daily list — holding the limit is the rule that makes the whole ritual work. As [Time Management for Developers](/en/posts/time-management-for-developers) covers, a handful of sharp goals gets more done than a long list of vague ones.

## How do you park everything else without it ballooning?

Write everything that didn't make the three outcomes onto a single someday list, and don't open that list again until next week's review — it's how you get ideas out of your head without losing them. Don't panic as the list grows; its job isn't to stay small, it's to hold things somewhere safe outside your head.

Review the someday list in one pass every quarter: promote anything still relevant into next week's three outcomes, delete the rest. Skip that quarterly cleanup and the someday list turns into a permanent source of guilt instead of a parking lot.

## What does the one-page template look like?

The one-page template has four sections: a date and 25-minute timer note at the top, a calendar summary (last week / next week) in the middle, a box for the three outcomes below that, and the someday list at the bottom. Paper, a notes app, or a plain table all work — the same four sections, any tool.

[Forte Labs' one-touch review guide](https://fortelabs.com/blog/the-one-touch-guide-to-doing-a-weekly-review/) shows that repeating these four sections in the same order every week automates the ritual and cuts decision fatigue — you stop thinking about how to do it and just think about what you find.

## How is a weekly review different from a quarterly brag doc?

A weekly review is a planning tool, not a performance evaluation; a quarterly brag doc is the opposite — a record kept to prove what you accomplished. Confusing the two turns the weekly ritual into a self-evaluation session that stretches from 25 minutes into an hour.

| | Weekly review | Quarterly brag doc |
|---|---|---|
| Purpose | Plan the week ahead | Document past achievement |
| Frequency | Every week | Once a quarter |
| Duration | 25 minutes | 1–2 hours |
| Tone | Neutral, operational | Self-evaluative, evidence-driven |
| Output | Three outcomes and a someday list | Input for a performance conversation |

The weekly ritual has no room for "how good was I this week" — only "what gets finished next week." That distinction is the single rule that keeps it inside 25 minutes.

My own take: people drop the weekly review not because of the time cost, but because they let self-evaluation creep into it — the moment you start asking "how well did I do," 25 minutes becomes 50, and the week after gets skipped entirely. ## Frequently Asked Questions

### What's the best day and time for a weekly review?

Friday afternoon and Sunday evening are the two least chaotic slots, because the week is either already done or hasn't started yet. A review done mid-week gets tangled up with unfinished work and doesn't give you a clean reset.

### What app should I use for a weekly review?

No special app is required — paper, a notes app, or a plain table all work as long as they hold the same four sections: inbox, calendar, three outcomes, and a someday list. The tool doesn't change the outcome; repeating the same fixed structure every week does.

### What happens if I pick more than three outcomes?

Picking more than three goals turns the weekly review back into a vague to-do list, and by week's end none of them feel clearly "done." Keeping the limit at three is the rule that makes each one actually get finished.

### What do I do when the someday list gets too long?

Instead of reopening the someday list every week, review it in a single pass once a quarter: promote anything still relevant into next week's three outcomes and delete the rest. Skip that quarterly cleanup and the list turns into an ever-growing source of guilt.
